AQA A-Level Business Studies (7132)

Real-World Examples Bank

A bank of businesses you can use in your essays. Each one tells you what the business shows, which models it connects to, and the kind of question it fits. Pick a few you're comfortable with from each topic — you don't need to know them all.

Why this matters
Where real-world examples earn you marks

Paper 1 essays (25 marks)

Completely open — no case study. Real-world examples are the only way to show application. The examiner praised students who used specific businesses in 2025 and 2026. Without them, it is very difficult to reach Level 4 or 5.

Paper 2 — "all businesses" questions (16 marks)

The final question in each section usually asks whether something applies to "all businesses." Responses using only the case study business are capped. You need different types of business — large vs small, B2B vs B2C, manufacturing vs service — to reach the top marks.

Paper 3 — Q6 (24 marks)

The synoptic essay at the end. The examiner confirmed in June 2026 that students who only used the case study business were capped at Level 4. You must use external businesses to reach the highest marks.

9-mark questions

Not essential, but a brief one-sentence reference to a real business can strengthen your argument. The examiner noted that "excellent examples of real-life innovation strategies" improved responses in 2025.

Competitive advantage
Porter's Generic Strategies and Five Forces. These examples help with any question about how businesses compete, maintain advantage, or respond to competitive pressure.
Cost leadership
Ryanair
Europe's largest low-cost airline. Keeps costs down through high capacity utilisation (fast 25-minute turnaround vs 45+ minutes at traditional airlines), one aircraft type (Boeing 737), secondary airports, no frills, and ancillary revenue from baggage and seat selection fees.
Porter's Generic Strategies Capacity utilisation NCA utilisation Pricing P1 essays · P2 Q3.3 2025
Aldi
Discount supermarket with roughly 800 product lines compared with 50,000 at Tesco. Own-label dominance keeps supplier costs low. Small store format reduces property costs. Administration costs around 15% of turnover vs 20% for the big four. Pays above-average wages but employs fewer staff per store — high labour productivity.
Porter's Generic Strategies Porter's Five Forces Barriers to entry HR efficiency P1 essays · P2 2017
Primark
Fast fashion at very low prices. No online shop — saves logistics and returns costs. High volume, low margin model. Sources from low-cost countries. After the Rana Plaza collapse in 2013, faced major ethical scrutiny over its supply chain.
Porter's Generic Strategies Ethics E-commerce Place decisions P1 essays
Differentiation
Apple
Differentiates through product design, ecosystem lock-in (iPhone, Mac, Watch, iCloud), and strong brand loyalty. Charges premium prices. R&D spending around $30bn per year. Customers show very price-inelastic demand because of brand loyalty and switching costs.
Porter's Generic Strategies Ansoff (product development) Innovation Brand loyalty PED P1 essays
Dyson
UK manufacturer that differentiates through innovation. Spent 15 years and 5,127 prototypes developing the bagless vacuum. Invests over £7m per week in R&D. Moved manufacturing to Malaysia to reduce costs while maintaining premium positioning.
Porter's Generic Strategies Innovation R&D investment Offshoring Entrepreneurship P1 essays
Rolls-Royce (cars)
Ultra-premium differentiation based on heritage, craftsmanship, and customisation — not innovation. Proves that differentiation does not always require innovation. Useful for challenging the assumption in Paper 3 2024 Q6 that innovation is essential for differentiation.
Porter's Generic Strategies Differentiation without innovation P3 Q6 2024
Five Forces in action
UK supermarket industry
High rivalry (price wars between Tesco, Sainsbury's, Aldi, Lidl). Low barriers to entry for online (Ocado, Amazon Fresh) but high for physical stores. High buyer power — customers switch easily. Low supplier power — supermarkets dictate terms to farmers.
Porter's Five Forces Rivalry Buyer power Supplier power P1 essays · P2 2017
Pharmaceutical industry
High barriers to entry (patents, regulation, R&D costs of £1bn+ per drug). Low buyer power — patients need specific drugs. When patents expire, generic competition is intense and prices fall sharply. Useful for showing how Five Forces change over time.
Porter's Five Forces Barriers to entry Patents Regulation as protection P1 essays
Growth strategies
Ansoff Matrix, organic vs external growth, integration, MNCs, and franchising. These examples cover how businesses choose to grow, the risks involved, and why growth strategies sometimes fail.
Ansoff Matrix
Coca-Cola — market penetration
Increases advertising and promotions to sell more of the same product to existing customers. Also uses seasonal campaigns and partnerships (Coca-Cola x McDonald's) to boost frequency of purchase. The least risky Ansoff strategy.
Ansoff Matrix Market penetration Low risk P1 essays
Apple Watch — product development
New product sold to existing customers already in the Apple ecosystem. Medium risk — requires R&D investment and the product might not succeed, but the customer base is already there.
Ansoff Matrix Product development P1 2018 essay
Aldi/Lidl entering the UK — market development
Expanded from Germany into the UK with the same low-cost model. Medium risk — existing product in a new market. Took decades of slow organic growth to build market share from nothing to a combined 18%+.
Ansoff Matrix Market development Organic growth P1 essays · P2 2017
Amazon — diversification
Moved from online retail into cloud computing (AWS — now its most profitable division), streaming (Prime Video), grocery (Whole Foods acquisition), and healthcare. The highest-risk Ansoff strategy, but Amazon's scale and cash reserves reduce the risk.
Ansoff Matrix Diversification Highest risk P1 essays
Integration
Netflix — backward vertical integration
Produces its own content rather than buying from studios. Reduces supplier power, secures exclusive content, and controls quality. Originally relied on other studios' content, now spends $17bn+ per year on original programming.
Porter's Five Forces Backward vertical integration Supplier power P1 2026 essay
Tesla — forward vertical integration
Sells directly to customers through its own showrooms and website, bypassing traditional car dealerships. Controls the customer experience and keeps the full margin. Faced legal challenges in some US states where direct selling was banned.
Forward vertical integration Distribution P1 2026 essay
Facebook buying Instagram — horizontal integration
Acquired a direct competitor in 2012 for $1bn. Increased market share in social media, reduced rivalry, and gained access to a younger demographic. Also acquired WhatsApp for $19bn in 2014.
Horizontal integration Market share P1 essays
MNCs and global expansion
Tesco — Fresh & Easy (USA failure)
Entered the US market in 2007, lost over £1bn, and withdrew by 2013. Did not understand American shopping habits — stores were too small, product range too narrow, branding did not connect. A clear example of why becoming an MNC does not guarantee profit.
Ansoff (market development) MNC failure Cultural differences P1 2025 essay · P2 2017
Unilever
Operates in 190+ countries. Adapts products to local markets — different Magnum flavours, different Dove formulations, smaller pack sizes in India. Both a production base and a consumer market in emerging economies. Useful for local responsiveness, MNC advantages, and economies of scale with adaptation.
MNCs Emerging markets Elkington's TBL Local adaptation P1 essays · P2 2017
McDonald's — franchising
Over 90% of McDonald's restaurants are franchises. Adapts menus to local tastes (McSpicy Paneer in India, Teriyaki Burger in Japan). The franchisor keeps control of quality and brand while the franchisee takes on the financial risk and local management.
Franchising Globalisation Cultural adaptation P2 2020 (Zani franchising Q)
Kraft / Cadbury takeover
Kraft took over Cadbury in 2010 despite opposition. Closed the Somerdale factory despite promising to keep it open. Job losses followed. Useful for stakeholder conflict in takeovers, cultural clash, and why external growth creates integration problems.
External growth Stakeholder conflict Culture clash P1 essays · P3 2022 (takeover Q)
HR, motivation and culture
Maslow, Herzberg, Taylor, Handy's cultures, hard and soft HRM. These examples help with any question about how businesses manage their people, motivate employees, or change their organisational culture.
Herzberg
Google
Free meals, on-site gyms, and sleep pods are hygiene factors — they prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate. The real motivation comes from the "20% time" policy (now evolved), where engineers work on their own projects. Gmail and Google Maps both started this way. Shows the distinction between hygiene factors and motivators clearly.

Remember: Pay is a hygiene factor in Herzberg's theory — it prevents dissatisfaction but does not motivate. This is the single most common error in AQA exams.
Herzberg Hygiene vs motivators Soft HRM P1 essays · P2 2025
Amazon warehouses
High pay relative to competitors (hygiene factor addressed), but reports of intense monitoring, limited autonomy, and repetitive tasks. High labour turnover despite good pay. Shows that addressing hygiene factors alone does not create motivation if motivators — achievement, recognition, responsibility — are absent.
Herzberg Taylor Hard HRM Labour turnover P1 essays · P2 2020 (Taylor Q)
John Lewis Partnership
Employee-owned — all staff are "partners" who share profits. Provides both hygiene factors (competitive pay, annual bonus) and motivators (sense of ownership, responsibility, involvement in decisions). Consistently lower labour turnover than competitors.
Herzberg Maslow Soft HRM Employee engagement P1 essays
Maslow
NHS during COVID
Physiological and safety needs were threatened — PPE shortages, infection risk. Even highly motivated healthcare workers found it difficult to focus on higher-level needs when basic safety was compromised. Shows the hierarchical nature of Maslow — you cannot skip levels.
Maslow Hierarchy of needs P1 2023 essay · P2
Taylor
Amazon fulfilment centres
Closely monitored, targets set per hour, performance-related consequences. Standardised processes and measurement. Reflects Taylor's scientific management. But also shows the limits of Taylor — high turnover, negative press, and difficulty attracting workers despite above-average pay.
Taylor Scientific management Piece rate logic P2 2020 (Taylor Q)
Handy's cultures
Any founder-led startup (power culture)
The founder makes all decisions. Fast decision-making but limits growth as the business gets bigger. As the business grows, shareholders often push for a shift to role culture with clearer procedures — exactly what happened with Zani PLC in the Paper 2 2020 case study.
Handy Power culture Culture change P2 2020 · P3 2022
Hard vs soft HRM
Costco (soft HRM)
Pays above-average wages, promotes from within, and has low labour turnover (around 6% vs 40%+ at some competitors). Treats employees as assets worth investing in. Higher individual costs but lower recruitment and training costs overall.
Soft HRM Herzberg Productivity P1 2024 essay
Sports Direct (hard HRM)
Zero-hours contracts, warehouse workers treated as costs to minimise. High turnover, negative publicity, and reputational damage. Useful for hard HRM, ethics of flexible contracts, and stakeholder conflict.
Hard HRM Zero-hours contracts Ethics P1 2022 essay · P2
Toyota (blend of both)
Kaizen culture involves workers in continuous improvement — a soft HRM element. But production targets are strict and processes are highly standardised — a hard HRM element. Shows that most real businesses use a mix of both approaches.
Hard and soft HRM Kaizen P1 essays
Operations
Lean production, JIT, quality (QA vs QC), capacity utilisation, capital vs labour intensive. These examples cover the operations questions that appear across all three papers.
Lean production and JIT
Toyota
Originated the Toyota Production System — lean, JIT, kaizen, kanban. Workers can stop the production line if they spot a defect (andon cord). Minimal inventory held. But the 2011 Japanese earthquake exposed the vulnerability of JIT — when supply chains broke, production halted globally for weeks.
Lean production JIT Kaizen Supply chain risk P1 2024 essay · P2 2020 · P2 2025
Rolls-Royce (aerospace)
Cannot fully use lean because of the bespoke, high-precision nature of jet engine manufacturing. Quality cannot be compromised, and each engine is essentially custom-built. Useful for arguing that lean does not suit every business.
Lean limitations Bespoke manufacturing P1 2024 essay
Quality assurance vs quality control
Boeing 737 MAX
Quality control failures led to two fatal crashes in 2018 and 2019. Investigations revealed cost-cutting had weakened quality processes. A powerful example of what happens when quality is compromised to reduce costs.
QC failure Cost vs quality trade-off P1 2025 essay
Capacity utilisation
Ryanair
Achieves very high capacity utilisation through 25-minute aircraft turnaround (vs 45+ at traditional airlines), single aircraft type, and point-to-point routes. Fixed costs spread over more flights per day means lower unit costs. Central to its cost leadership strategy.
High capacity utilisation NCA utilisation Unit costs P2 2025 Q3.3 · P2 2017
Tesla (early years)
Struggled with low capacity utilisation during "production hell" in 2018 when ramping up Model 3 production. High fixed costs spread over low output meant very high unit costs. Shows that capacity utilisation problems can threaten even well-funded businesses during growth.
Low capacity utilisation Scaling challenges P1 essays · P3 2024
Capital-intensive vs labour-intensive
Amazon fulfilment centres (capital-intensive)
Increasingly automated — robots picking and packing. Reduces labour costs per unit, increases speed and accuracy. But requires massive upfront investment in technology.
Capital-intensive Technology investment P1 2026 essay
Care homes, restaurants (labour-intensive)
Cannot easily replace human interaction with machinery. The person IS the product. Labour costs form a high proportion of turnover. Useful for arguing that some industries must remain labour-intensive.
Labour-intensive Service sector P1 2026 essay
Marketing
Marketing mix (7Ps), segmentation, positioning, branding, and elasticity. These examples cover any question about how businesses reach customers and compete in their markets.
Marketing mix (7Ps)
Nike
Product: continuous innovation (Vaporfly, Air Max, recovery shoes). Price: premium pricing enabled by strong brand. Promotion: celebrity endorsement and "Just Do It." Place: direct-to-consumer via Nike app and own stores, reducing reliance on third-party retailers. People and Physical environment in Nike Town flagship stores. Shows all 7Ps working together.
7Ps Differentiation Brand loyalty P1 2026 essay
ASOS
Online-only fashion retailer. Place is everything — distribution and delivery speed are the competitive advantage. Useful for place/distribution as the most important element of the mix, and for e-commerce strategy.
Place / distribution E-commerce P1 2019 essay · P1 2022 essay
Segmentation and positioning
Rolex vs Casio
Both sell watches but target completely different segments. Rolex: high income, prestige, very price-inelastic demand. Casio: mass market, functional, price-sensitive. Useful for segmentation by income, market mapping, and contrasting positioning strategies.
Segmentation Market mapping PED P2 2025 Q2.4
Elasticity
Supermarket own-brand basics (negative YED)
When incomes rise, people switch to branded products. When incomes fall, demand for own-brand basics increases. An inferior good with negative income elasticity. This is the concept students struggle with most on MCQs — understanding that demand rises when incomes fall.
Negative YED Inferior goods P1 MCQ Q11 2026 · P1 essays
Luxury goods (positive YED > 1)
Designer handbags, premium cars, fine dining. Demand rises more than proportionally when incomes rise and falls sharply in recession. Income elasticity greater than +1 — luxury goods. Useful for showing how economic environment affects different businesses differently.
Positive YED Luxury goods P1 essays · P2 2020
Finance
Ratios, investment appraisal, gearing, cash flow, and debt factoring. These examples help with any question about how businesses manage their finances, fund growth, or measure performance.
Gearing and financial risk
Debenhams
Collapsed in 2020 with high debt levels. Unable to service debt when revenue fell. Creditors lost out, employees lost jobs. A clear example of what happens when high gearing meets falling sales. Also useful for limited liability — shareholders lost their investment but not personal assets, while creditors and employees suffered more.
High gearing Cash flow failure Stakeholder conflict Limited liability P1 2020 essay · P3 2026
Cash flow vs profit
Carillion
Collapsed in 2018 despite having a £5bn order book. Profitable on paper but ran out of cash. Long payment terms (90+ days to subcontractors), upfront material costs, and thin margins meant cash flow could not keep up. The most important distinction students must understand for Paper 3.
Cash flow vs profit Working capital P3 2026 (Danni cash flow Q)
Investment appraisal
Amazon
Ran at a loss for years, prioritising long-term investment over short-term profit. Invested heavily in warehouses, technology, and AWS infrastructure. NPV of many projects was positive over a long time horizon but negative in the short term. Shows why ARR and payback can be misleading for long-term strategic investments.
Investment appraisal Long-term vs short-term NPV P1 essays · P2 2020 · P3
HS2
UK infrastructure project with costs escalating from £32bn to over £100bn. Demonstrates how investment appraisal assumptions — costs, timelines, demand forecasts — can prove inaccurate. Useful for sensitivity analysis and the limitations of quantitative investment appraisal.
Sensitivity analysis Investment appraisal limitations P1 essays · P3
Ethics, CSR and legislation
Carroll's CSR Pyramid, Elkington's Triple Bottom Line, and the debate over whether businesses act responsibly without being forced to. These examples cover any question about ethics, environment, or legislation.
Carroll's CSR Pyramid
Patagonia
Operates at the philanthropic level — donates 1% of revenue to environmental causes, ran a "Don't Buy This Jacket" campaign, and transferred ownership to an environmental trust. Proves that strong CSR can be a competitive advantage, not just a cost.
Carroll's CSR Pyramid Elkington's TBL CSR as advantage P1 2017 essay · P2 2023
BP — Deepwater Horizon
The 2010 oil spill caused massive environmental and reputational damage. BP met its economic and legal responsibilities but ethical and philanthropic behaviour was questioned. Cost BP over $65bn in fines, clean-up, and compensation. Useful for stakeholder management, reputation, and the cost of ethical failure.
Carroll's CSR Pyramid Stakeholder conflict Reputation P1 essays · P2 2023
Primark — Rana Plaza
The 2013 factory collapse in Bangladesh killed over 1,100 garment workers. Primark sourced from the building. Led to significant changes in supply chain auditing. Useful for ethics, supply chain responsibility, and whether businesses only act responsibly when forced to by public pressure.
Supply chain ethics Stakeholder pressure P2 2023 Q3.4
Legislation as opportunity
Tesla
Benefits from environmental legislation. Government subsidies for EVs, emission standards that penalise traditional manufacturers — all create demand for Tesla's products. Legislation is an opportunity, not a threat. Useful for challenging "legislation is always damaging" statements.
Legislation as opportunity Environmental regulation P2 2024 Q3.3 · P3 2025 · P3 2026
Strategic change
Strategic drift, contingency planning, change management (Kotter & Schlesinger), and leadership. These examples cover the strategic questions that dominate the higher-mark essays.
Strategic drift
Nokia
Was the world's largest mobile phone manufacturer. Failed to respond to the smartphone revolution led by Apple's iPhone in 2007. Continued making button phones while the market shifted to touchscreens. The external environment changed dramatically but Nokia's strategy did not — a textbook case of strategic drift.
Strategic drift Failure to adapt P1 2024 essay
Blockbuster
Dominated video rental. Turned down the opportunity to buy Netflix for $50m in 2000. Did not adapt to streaming or digital distribution. Closed almost all stores by 2014. Useful for strategic drift, failure to respond to digital disruption, and contingency planning failure.
Strategic drift Digital disruption P1 2024 essay · P1 2017 essay
Contingency planning
BA IT failure (2017)
A power supply issue caused a global IT system failure. Thousands of flights cancelled, 75,000 passengers stranded. BA was criticised for not having adequate contingency plans. Shows that even large businesses can be caught out by events they should have planned for.
Contingency planning IT failure P1 2023 essay · P2 2018
COVID-19
Tested contingency plans across every industry. Airlines grounded fleets, retailers shifted to online, restaurants pivoted to takeaway. Businesses with flexible operations and strong cash reserves survived better. Useful for the limits of planning for unprecedented events.
Contingency planning Unprecedented events P1 2023 essay
Leadership
Elon Musk / Tesla
Visionary, autocratic leadership. Drives innovation through personal involvement and ambitious targets. But critics point to high staff turnover, unpredictable decision-making, and burnout culture. Useful for whether strong leadership is essential for growth, and the trade-offs of autocratic styles.
Leadership Tannenbaum-Schmidt P3 2023 Q6 · P1 essays
Tesco under Terry Leahy vs later CEOs
Leahy oversaw Tesco's rise to the UK's largest retailer. After his departure, the business experienced strategic drift, an accounting scandal, and loss of market share. Shows that leadership matters — and that leadership succession is a genuine strategic risk.
Leadership succession Strategic drift P3 2023 Q6
Digital technology and innovation
E-commerce, digital disruption, and innovation strategy. These examples cover questions about how technology changes the way businesses compete.
Netflix
Started as DVD-by-post. Adapted to streaming before competitors did. Now produces its own content — backward vertical integration. Uses data analytics to decide what content to make. A business that successfully responded to change rather than drifting.
Digital transformation Emergent strategy Adaptation P1 2017 essay · P1 essays
Uber
Disrupted the taxi industry through app-based technology. Flexible employment model (gig economy) — drivers are independent contractors, not employees. Faced regulatory challenges globally. Useful for digital disruption, flexible contracts, ethics, and legislation.
Digital disruption Gig economy Legislation P1 2022 essay · P2 2019
3M (intrapreneurship)
Allows employees to spend 15% of their time on personal projects. Post-it Notes were invented this way. Useful for intrapreneurship, innovation culture, and Herzberg's motivators through autonomy and achievement.
Intrapreneurship Innovation culture Herzberg (motivators) P1 9-markers · P1 essays
Kodak
Invented the digital camera but chose not to develop it because film was more profitable. When digital eventually disrupted the market, Kodak was too slow. Filed for bankruptcy in 2012. The classic example of a business that had the technology but not the strategic will to use it.
Innovation failure Strategic drift Short-term vs long-term P1 2017 essay · P1 2024 essay
External environment
Interest rates, exchange rates, fiscal policy, protectionism, and globalisation. These examples help with any question about how the wider economy affects businesses.
Interest rates
UK housebuilders (Barratt, Persimmon)
Very sensitive to interest rate changes. Higher rates mean more expensive mortgages, which directly reduces demand for new homes. Also affects their own borrowing costs for land purchases. Sales volume and profit can swing significantly with even a 0.5% rate change.
Interest rates Demand sensitivity P1 9-markers · P1 essays
Exchange rates
JLR (Jaguar Land Rover)
UK-based manufacturer owned by Tata (India). Sells heavily in China and the US. A strong pound makes its cars more expensive abroad, reducing demand. A weak pound boosts competitiveness but increases the cost of imported components. Useful for exchange rate impact on exporters.
Exchange rates Exports MNCs P1 9-markers · P2 2018 · P2 2025
Protectionism
UK steel industry
Has benefited from tariffs on cheap Chinese steel imports. Without protection, UK steel producers would struggle to compete on price. Shows that protectionism can save domestic industries — at least in the short term.
Protectionism Tariffs P1 2018 essay
Nissan Sunderland / Brexit
Brexit created uncertainty about tariff barriers with the EU. Nissan publicly considered whether to continue UK production. Eventually stayed, but the uncertainty affected investment decisions. Useful for protectionism, trade barriers, and MNC location decisions.
Protectionism Trade barriers MNC decisions P1 2018 essay
Globalisation and emerging markets
Coca-Cola in Africa
One of the first Western brands to invest heavily in Africa. Adapted distribution to reach rural areas using local distributors with bicycles and handcarts. Shows both the opportunities (growing population, rising incomes) and challenges (infrastructure, distribution) of emerging markets.
Emerging markets Distribution Adaptation P1 2023 essay · P2 2017
Shein
Chinese fast-fashion company that grew globally through social media marketing and ultra-low prices. Criticised for labour practices, environmental impact, and intellectual property concerns. Useful for globalisation, ethics in supply chains, and how digital marketing enables rapid international expansion.
Globalisation Ethics Digital marketing P1 essays · P2
Exam technique
How to use these in your essays

Keep it brief

You are not writing about the business — you are using it to support your argument. One or two sentences is enough.

Good
"This is similar to what happened at Toyota, where lean production reduced waste and lowered unit costs, but the 2011 earthquake exposed the risk of minimal inventory when supply chains are disrupted."
Too long

A full paragraph describing Toyota's history, the Toyota Production System, Taiichi Ohno's philosophy, the earthquake, and the recovery. The examiner does not need a case study within your case study.

Make it do a job

Every example should either support your argument or provide a contrast.

For analysis
"For example, Ryanair achieves very high capacity utilisation through fast aircraft turnaround, which spreads fixed costs across more flights and reduces unit costs. This suggests that for low-cost businesses, maximising NCA use is central to their strategy."
For evaluation
"However, this may not apply to all businesses. A luxury hotel, for instance, might deliberately run at lower capacity to maintain exclusivity and justify premium pricing."

Use different types of business

For "all businesses" questions, the examiner wants to see that you can think beyond one type. The strongest answers contrast:

Large vs small · Manufacturing vs service · B2B vs B2C · UK vs international · Luxury vs budget · Established vs startup

Connect to models

The most effective use of an example is when it is woven into a model.

Weak
"Aldi uses cost leadership."
Strong
"Aldi's competitive advantage comes from cost leadership, which, according to Porter, is sustained through barriers to entry — in Aldi's case, the difficulty for competitors of replicating its lean operating model with only 800 product lines and minimal staff per store."
