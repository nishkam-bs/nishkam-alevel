Key Formulae — AQA’s Operations Calculations

Formulae You MUST Know
Labour productivity = Total output ÷ Number of employees
Unit cost (average cost) = Total costs ÷ Total output
Capacity utilisation (%) = (Actual output ÷ Maximum capacity) × 100
Employee costs as % of turnover = (Total employee costs ÷ Turnover) × 100
Labour turnover (%) = (Number of leavers ÷ Average number of employees) × 100
Labour retention rate (%) = 100% − Labour turnover %

Examiner Trap #1 — Capacity Utilisation MCQs (Tested 2017 AND 2020)
2020 P1 Q3: “An increase in capacity utilisation allows more time for equipment maintenance” = FALSE. Higher utilisation means LESS downtime. “An increase allows meeting unexpected demand” = FALSE. Higher utilisation = LESS spare capacity. LOWER utilisation provides these benefits. Both 2017 P1 Q3 and 2020 P1 Q3 test this identical principle — that spare capacity has BENEFITS, not just costs.
Examiner Trap #2 — The Two-Objective MCQ (2025 P1 Q10)
Four factories, two objectives: min 80% capacity utilisation AND min 2 units/employee. You must calculate BOTH metrics for every factory. Only Factory C (80/100 = 80% ✓ and 80/40 = 2.0 ✓) meets both. Students who only check one objective get trapped. Answer: C.
3.4.3 Efficiency, Lean Production, JIT vs JIC & Resource Mix

How to Increase Efficiency & Labour Productivity

Training — upskilling workers to produce more/better output per hour. Technology / automation — replacing manual tasks with machinery or software. Better motivation — engaged workers produce more (links to Herzberg). Improved processes — lean production, waste elimination. Division of labour — workers specialise in what they do best. Investment in capital equipment — more/better machinery per worker.

Difficulties of Increasing Efficiency

Cost: New technology and training require significant upfront investment. Resistance to change: Employees may fear automation replacing their jobs. Short-term disruption: New systems reduce output during installation and learning. Quality risk: Pushing for higher speed/volume may compromise quality. Diminishing returns: After easy gains, further improvement becomes increasingly expensive.

Lean Production — JIT vs JIC

Just in Time (JIT)Just in Case (JIC)
WhatMaterials delivered exactly when needed. Zero/minimal inventory held.Large quantities held in stock “just in case.” Buffer stocks maintained.
AdvantagesLower storage costs; less waste/spoilage; improved cash flow; forces supplier quality; reduces obsolescence riskNever run out; can meet unexpected demand surges; bulk-buying discounts; less reliant on single supplier
DisadvantagesVulnerable to supply disruption; requires 100% reliable suppliers; more frequent deliveries; no buffer for demand spikesHigh storage/warehousing costs; cash tied up; risk of obsolescence; waste if demand falls; opportunity cost of capital
Best forReliable supply chains, predictable demand, perishable goods, limited storage, high-value componentsUnreliable supply chains, volatile demand, non-perishable goods, ample storage, low-value items
Examiner Trap #3 — “Lean Production Reduces Costs by Buying in Bulk” = FALSE
Tested directly in 2020 P1 Q5. Statement 1: “Lean production reduces unit costs because it involves buying raw materials in greater bulk” = FALSE. Lean/JIT does the OPPOSITE — smaller, more frequent deliveries. Statement 2: “Lean production reduces unit costs because it reduces wastage of raw materials” = TRUE. Answer: C (S1 false, S2 true).
Real-World Examples for Essays
JIT success + failure — Toyota: Pioneered JIT (Toyota Production System), reducing inventory costs by billions. But the 2011 Japanese tsunami disrupted suppliers and halted global production — no buffer stock to fall back on. Use this for BOTH sides of JIT arguments.

COVID-19 exposed JIT: Car manufacturers couldn’t get semiconductor chips; retailers ran out of basics. Many businesses shifted back toward JIC. This is the definitive example for “lean production doesn’t always work” essay arguments (2024 P1 Q24).

Seebus (2025 P2 Q2.2): Uses JIT for bus manufacturing. Buses are large, expensive assets — holding components in stock would require huge warehouses and tie up enormous amounts of cash. JIT minimises both.

Examiner Trap #4 — More Capital Intensive ≠ Economies of Scale (2019 P1 Q3)
A business replaces workers with machinery → 10% increase in capacity AND output, but unit costs rise 5%. This is more capital intensive but the RISING unit costs show diseconomies of scale — perhaps from higher maintenance costs, underutilised expensive machines, or transition inefficiencies. Answer: B.

Examiner Trap #5 — Quality + PED MCQs (Tested 2022 AND 2024)
2022 P1 Q10: “Improving quality makes demand more price elastic” = FALSE. Better quality creates brand loyalty and differentiation → demand becomes more INELASTIC. “Improving quality increases added value” = TRUE. Answer: C.

2024 P1 Q11: “Improving quality makes demand more price inelastic” = TRUE. “Improving quality leads to competitive advantage” = TRUE. Answer: A (both true). Improving quality → inelastic demand → competitive advantage. Memorise this chain.

Consequences of Poor Quality

Direct costs: Returns, refunds, warranty claims, product recalls, compensation. Reputation damage: Negative reviews, social media backlash, loss of brand trust. Lost sales: Customers switch to competitors. Legal action: Product liability claims, regulatory fines. Demotivation: Workers take less pride in work.

Real-World Examples for Essays
QA success — Toyota: Toyota’s Kaizen and Jidoka systems are textbook QA — every worker can stop the production line if they spot a defect. This minimises defects and reduces waste. Use for QA essay arguments.

Poor quality consequences — Samsung Galaxy Note 7 (2016): Battery defects caused phones to catch fire. Samsung recalled 2.5 million units, lost an estimated $5 billion, and suffered severe reputational damage. Quality failures can be existential.

QC still has value — Food industry: QC (final inspection, batch testing, microbiological checks) is legally required regardless of QA systems. This is the counter-argument: QA doesn’t replace QC in regulated industries.

Examiner Trap #6 — Re-Order Level Calculation (2018 P1 Q15)
Buffer = 250 units. Usage = 50 units/day. Lead time = 5 days.
Re-order level = (5 × 50) + 250 = 250 + 250 = 500 units.
Students who forget to ADD the buffer stock get 250 (wrong). The buffer exists precisely so stock doesn’t hit zero during lead time — it MUST be included.

Examiner Trap #7 — “Reliability Is Most Important” (2022 P2 Q3.6)
This 16-mark question asks “for all businesses.” The strong answer: reliability is MOST important for JIT businesses — one late delivery = production stops. But for businesses using JIC or with ample buffer stock, price may be MORE important. For luxury brands, quality outweighs reliability because a brand-damaging defective input is worse than a late delivery. It depends on the business model, industry, and inventory strategy.

Essay 1: “Will lean production always lead to lower unit costs?” [25 marks] — 2024 P1 Q24

Essay Structure Guide

Structure: Introduction → Argument For → Balanced Reply → Argument Against → Balanced Reply → Conclusion

Introduction — Standard Version

Lean production is a philosophy centred on eliminating waste from every stage of the production process — waste of materials, time, effort, and overproduction. The claim that this “always” leads to lower unit costs must be tested, because while lean reduces many costs, it introduces others and does not work equally in all business contexts.

Introduction — PBA Challenge Version

P: One could argue that lean production will always lead to lower unit costs because eliminating waste at every stage of the production process directly reduces material, storage and labour inefficiency. B: However, the implementation costs of lean production and its vulnerability to supply chain disruption may in fact raise unit costs in certain business environments, as demonstrated by the semiconductor crisis of 2021–2022 which exposed the fragility of JIT systems (Lean Production). A: Ultimately, this essay will argue that lean production is likely to reduce unit costs for large-volume manufacturers with stable supply chains, but the word “always” is too absolute, as the outcome depends heavily on the reliability of the external environment and the scale of implementation costs.

Argument One (FOR) — Version A: Explicit Chains of Analysis

One could argue that lean production will lead to lower unit costs because its core mechanism is the systematic elimination of waste. This means that by producing only what is needed, when it is needed through JIT systems (Lean Production / JIT), a business avoids the cost of holding excess inventory in warehouses, eliminates spoilage of perishable materials, and removes the overhead of managing unsold finished goods. Therefore, storage and inventory holding costs fall directly, as a result reducing total operational costs per unit produced. This could lead to a strengthened competitive position as the business can pass savings on as lower prices, attracting price-sensitive consumers and growing market share, or retain the savings as higher profit margins. This can be explored through the success of Toyota PLC, which pioneered its Toyota Production System (TPS) — a lean approach based on waste elimination (Muda) and continuous improvement (Kaizen) (Lean Production). Toyota’s unit costs fell significantly relative to competitors, allowing it to price competitively whilst maintaining strong margins and becoming a long-term Cash Cow on the (Boston Matrix). Furthermore, lean’s “right first time” approach to quality assurance (Quality Assurance) reduces the cost of reworking defective products, handling returns, and managing warranty claims, causing unit costs to fall from multiple directions simultaneously — materials, storage and quality failures. This thus leads to the conclusion that for high-volume manufacturers with reliable supply chains and stable, predictable demand, lean production consistently delivers lower unit costs and a durable cost-leadership competitive advantage (Porter’s Generic Strategies).

Balanced Reply to Argument One

However, this may not be the case if the business is in the early stages of lean implementation, as transitioning from traditional JIC to JIT requires retraining the entire workforce, restructuring supplier relationships, and investing in monitoring systems — during which unit costs often RISE before they fall. This is likely to depend on the size and resources of the business: large corporations like Toyota can absorb implementation costs over years, whereas smaller businesses may never recover these upfront investments, meaning lean actually raises long-term unit costs. However, this assumes that lean implementation runs smoothly, when in practice cultural resistance from employees who fear redundancy through automation can significantly delay efficiency gains.

Argument Two (AGAINST) — Version A: Explicit Chains of Analysis

On the other hand, one could argue that lean production does not always lead to lower unit costs because JIT’s reliance on frequent, perfectly timed deliveries creates acute supply chain vulnerability. This means that when supply chains are disrupted — through natural disasters, geopolitical events, or pandemics — JIT businesses face production stoppages that cost far more than maintaining buffer stock would have done. Therefore, the hidden costs of supply disruption must be factored into lean’s true unit cost calculation, as a result revealing that in volatile external environments, the savings on storage are outweighed by the losses from production downtime. This could lead to a fundamental reconsideration of lean production’s suitability, as demonstrated by global car manufacturers during the 2021–2022 semiconductor shortage, where JIT adoption meant factories sat idle for months, costing billions in lost production — a vastly greater cost than warehousing chips would have been. Furthermore, lean production is ill-suited to businesses with highly variable or seasonal demand patterns — a fashion retailer launching new collections, for example, requires significant inventory pre-season, making JIT scheduling impractical and potentially more expensive than JIC alternatives. Causing the additional costs of emergency ordering, expedited freight and production delays to erode — or even eliminate — any unit cost savings previously achieved. This thus leads to the recognition that lean production’s unit cost benefits are conditional upon a stable, reliable external environment — precisely the condition that is increasingly difficult to guarantee in a globalised, post-pandemic world.

Balanced Reply to Argument Two

However, the impact may be limited because many businesses now adopt hybrid inventory strategies — lean principles combined with targeted buffer stocks for the most vulnerable components — preserving most of lean’s cost advantages whilst mitigating the worst supply disruption risks. This could be less significant if the business operates with multiple, geographically diversified suppliers, reducing dependency on any single supply chain node and maintaining JIT’s cost benefits even under moderate external disruption. Therefore, the success of this will depend on the sophistication of the business’s supply chain management and its ability to identify and hedge against its most critical supply chain vulnerabilities through scenario planning.

Conclusion

Overall, one could argue that lean production is a powerful driver of lower unit costs for businesses that operate in stable, predictable environments with reliable supplier networks.

While both arguments have merit, the most significant factor that needs to be considered is supply chain reliability, as its impact outweighs implementation costs in terms of scale and unpredictability, particularly in the context of an increasingly volatile global trading environment characterised by geopolitical risk and climate disruption.

However, this depends on the industry and scale of operation, because high-volume standardised manufacturers like Toyota and Nissan operate sufficiently diversified supply chains to manage disruption risks, whereas smaller businesses in niche sectors remain acutely exposed. One could also argue that it depends on the time horizon — lean reduces unit costs in the long run, but during implementation and disruption events, costs may rise.

But ultimately, the supply chain vulnerability argument carries greater weight than the waste-elimination argument in the current macroeconomic context, meaning that the unit cost benefits of lean production are real but conditional — not guaranteed. The word “always” is therefore too absolute. Therefore, lean production will lead to lower unit costs for most large-volume manufacturers under normal conditions, but businesses in volatile markets or with fragile supply chains should adopt hybrid approaches rather than pure JIT.

Same Essay — Chains of Analysis Present but Not Explicitly Signposted

Introduction

However, the impact may be limited because sophisticated businesses increasingly adopt hybrid inventory models that preserve lean’s efficiency principles whilst maintaining targeted buffer stocks for the most supply-critical components. This could be less significant if the business builds sufficiently diversified supplier networks, ensuring no single disruption event can halt production, and thereby retaining most of JIT’s unit cost advantages alongside supply resilience.

While both arguments carry analytical weight, the most significant factor that needs to be considered is the stability of the external supply environment, as its impact outweighs all other considerations to a greater extent — particularly given the post-pandemic shift toward supply chain fragility that no longer represents an exceptional risk but a structural feature of global trade.

However, this depends on the sector, because Toyota and Nissan’s supply chain sophistication means disruption risks are manageable, while smaller businesses in narrow sectors remain acutely exposed to single points of failure. But ultimately, the supply chain vulnerability argument carries greater weight than waste elimination in the current environment, meaning lean production’s cost benefits are real but conditional. Therefore, the word “always” is too absolute — lean production will reduce unit costs in favourable conditions, but hybrid strategies represent the optimal approach where external volatility is significant.

Essay 2: “QA will always lead to greater profits than QC.” Do you agree? [25 marks] — 2025 P1 Q24

Essay Structure Guide

Structure: Introduction → Argument For → Balanced Reply → Argument Against → Balanced Reply → Conclusion

Introduction — Standard Version

Quality assurance prevents defects by embedding quality into every production stage, while quality control detects defects through inspection after production. The claim that QA “always” generates higher profits than QC requires analysis of both the revenue and cost implications of each method, and whether “always” holds across different business contexts.

Introduction — PBA Challenge Version

P: One could argue that quality assurance will always lead to greater profits than quality control because preventing defects at every stage of production eliminates waste costs more effectively than inspecting for defects after resources have already been consumed. B: However, the significant upfront investment required to implement QA — including workforce training, cultural transformation, and process redesign — may depress profits substantially in the short to medium term, with payback periods potentially extending beyond a business’s planning horizon. A: Ultimately, this essay will argue that QA is more likely to generate greater profits than QC for businesses producing high-value or branded products where quality directly influences consumer loyalty and pricing power, but the word “always” cannot be sustained across all industries, scales, and time horizons.

Argument One (FOR QA) — Version A: Explicit Chains of Analysis

One could argue that quality assurance consistently leads to greater profits than quality control because preventing defects is fundamentally more cost-efficient than detecting them after production. This means that under QA, materials, labour and energy are not consumed to produce defective goods, eliminating the cost of scrapping, reworking and reprocessing that QC necessarily incurs after defects have already been created. Therefore, total production costs fall on a per-unit basis, as a result directly improving profit margins for every unit sold without requiring any increase in selling price. This could lead to a reinforcing cycle of competitive advantage: lower defect rates improve brand reputation, which increases customer loyalty and pricing power (Price Elasticity of Demand), enabling premium pricing that further expands margins. This can be explored through the experience of Toyota PLC, whose Kaizen and Jidoka QA systems (Quality Assurance) reduced defect rates to near-zero, simultaneously cutting reworking costs and building Toyota’s reputation for exceptional reliability — a brand premium that allowed price positioning above many competitors and sustained the company as a global Cash Cow (Boston Matrix). Furthermore, QA’s empowerment of every worker to take responsibility for quality aligns with Herzberg’s motivator factors of achievement and responsibility, causing improvements in employee engagement and labour productivity that further reduce unit costs and improve profit performance. This thus leads to the conclusion that for high-volume manufacturers and premium brands where quality is a core competitive differentiator, QA generates greater profits than QC through dual mechanisms of cost reduction and revenue enhancement — a compounding profit advantage that QC cannot replicate.

Balanced Reply to Argument One

However, this may not be the case if the business is transitioning from QC to QA, as implementation costs — retraining the entire workforce, redesigning production processes, embedding new cultural norms — are substantial upfront investments that depress profitability for months or years before benefits materialise. This is likely to depend on the financial resources available to fund the transition: large PLCs with strong cash reserves can absorb implementation costs, whereas SMEs with limited capital may find that QC remains more profitable in the short to medium term simply because they cannot afford QA implementation without compromising liquidity. However, this assumes that QA implementation always succeeds — when cultural resistance prevents workers from genuinely accepting quality responsibility, QA investment yields minimal returns while ongoing QC costs continue.

Argument Two (AGAINST) — Version A: Explicit Chains of Analysis

On the other hand, one could argue that QC may generate greater profits than QA in specific business contexts, because in industries where final inspection is legally mandated, QA cannot replace QC — it can only supplement it. This means that pharmaceutical companies, food manufacturers, and aviation businesses must invest in both QA processes and QC inspection simultaneously, therefore the total cost of quality management is additive rather than substitutive, as a result meaning QA adoption increases total costs rather than reducing them in regulated sectors. This could lead to lower profits compared to simply optimising a well-designed QC system, particularly for businesses producing standardised, low-value goods where defect consequences are minimal and QA investment would be disproportionate. Consider basic packaging manufacturers: a misprinted cardboard box is scrapped at negligible cost; implementing a full TQM quality assurance system to prevent occasional misprints would cost vastly more than the defects it eliminates, meaning QC spot-checks generate greater profit by matching quality investment to the actual risk profile of the product. Furthermore, QC provides an objective, data-driven audit trail that external stakeholders — regulators, institutional investors, and major supply chain partners — can independently verify, causing QC-reliant businesses in regulated sectors to benefit from enhanced credibility and access to markets that QA-only approaches cannot fully satisfy. This thus leads to the recognition that the profit superiority of QA over QC is highly context-dependent, and the optimal quality strategy is determined by product value, regulatory environment, and defect consequence severity rather than a universal rule.

Balanced Reply to Argument Two

However, the impact may be limited because even in regulated industries, QA reduces the frequency and severity of defects that QC then inspects for, meaning both systems working together generate greater profits than QC alone — the argument against QA is not that it is inferior, but that it is insufficient as a standalone solution. This could be less significant if the business adopts a targeted QA approach focused on the highest-risk production stages, minimising QA investment whilst achieving disproportionate defect reduction benefits and keeping QC as a final safety net. However, other factors such as market positioning may have a greater impact: a business pursuing a differentiation strategy (Porter’s Generic Strategies) in a premium segment will always find QA more profitable than QC because quality is central to its price premium and brand equity.

Conclusion

Overall, one could argue that quality assurance generates greater profits than quality control for the majority of businesses producing high-value, complex, or branded products where quality is central to competitive positioning.

While both arguments have validity, the most significant factor that needs to be considered is the gap between a business’s current defect rate and its customer quality expectations, as its impact outweighs implementation cost considerations to a greater extent, particularly for businesses where poor quality triggers product liability claims, regulatory sanction, or brand reputation damage that is disproportionately costly relative to QA investment.

However, this depends on the regulatory environment, because in pharmaceuticals, food production, and aviation, QA supplements rather than replaces QC — making the “either/or” framing of the question inapplicable. It also depends on the time horizon: QA is less profitable in the short term due to implementation costs, but more profitable over a 5–10 year window as defect prevention compounds. But ultimately, the revenue and cost benefits of QA carry greater weight than QC’s simplicity advantages for most modern businesses, meaning the profit premium from brand loyalty and pricing power through QA exceeds QC’s lower implementation cost in all but the most commoditised or legally constrained contexts. Therefore, QA will lead to greater profits than QC in most scenarios, but “always” is too absolute — product type, industry regulation, and business scale critically determine which approach delivers superior profit performance.

16-Mark Evaluation: “Reliability is the most important factor in choice of supplier” — for all businesses? [16 marks] — 2022 P2

16-Mark Structure: For → Against → Balanced Reply → Conclusion

Argument FOR (Reliability IS Most Important)

One could argue that reliability is the most important factor when selecting a supplier because for businesses operating JIT inventory management systems (Lean Production / JIT), a late or missed delivery does not merely cause inconvenience — it triggers a complete production stoppage. This means that when a JIT manufacturer’s supplier fails to deliver on time, the entire production line grinds to a halt as there are no buffer stocks to draw upon, therefore every hour of production stoppage represents a direct, quantifiable loss of output, revenue, and fixed cost recovery, as a result making reliability a mission-critical supplier attribute that outweighs price, quality, and flexibility in these operational contexts. This could lead to long-term financial damage that far exceeds any cost savings from choosing a cheaper but less reliable supplier — as demonstrated by Toyota’s experience following the 2011 tsunami, where supplier unreliability forced global production stoppages costing billions in lost output. Furthermore, in supply chains serving time-sensitive sectors — perishable food retail, just-in-time automotive, or on-demand e-commerce fulfilment — a single unreliable supplier can trigger customer cancellations, lost contracts, and reputational damage that compounds the direct production loss. For these businesses, reliability is unambiguously the paramount supplier criterion.

Argument AGAINST (Reliability Is NOT Always Most Important)

On the other hand, one might suggest that for many businesses, price represents the most significant supplier selection criterion, because in commoditised, cost-leadership markets, supplier cost is the primary determinant of competitive positioning. A supermarket retailer pursuing a low-cost strategy (Porter’s Generic Strategies) operating large JIC buffer stocks can absorb occasional delivery delays without production disruption, meaning reliability is valuable but not critical — whereas a 5% reduction in supplier cost per unit, multiplied across millions of units annually, directly and substantially improves the retailer’s competitive pricing and profit margins. For luxury brands such as Burberry or Rolls-Royce, quality represents the paramount supplier criterion: a single sub-standard input component can compromise the brand integrity upon which premium pricing — and hence profitability — depends. A delayed delivery is recoverable through safety stock; a quality failure is potentially existential to brand reputation. Furthermore, for businesses sourcing from ethical or environmental certification supply chains, supplier sustainability credentials may outweigh reliability in terms of regulatory compliance, consumer trust, and long-term brand equity, particularly as ESG-linked consumer behaviour intensifies. It is therefore clear that the most important factor in supplier selection is determined by the business’s operational strategy, inventory model, market positioning, and product type — not by a universal rule.

Balanced Reply & Evaluation

However, the impact of reliability may be limited because even businesses with JIC inventory systems benefit from supply chain stability: frequent, unexpected deliveries from unreliable suppliers create hidden costs in administrative management, quality inspection, and renegotiation that cumulatively erode margins. This is likely to depend on the competitive intensity of the market and the degree of product differentiation: in markets with intense price competition and undifferentiated products (Porter’s Five Forces), cost leadership demands that price takes precedence over reliability. However, other factors such as the switching cost of changing suppliers may have a greater impact than any single supplier attribute — a business locked into a long-term supplier relationship may prioritise relationship quality and flexibility over pure reliability metrics.

Overall, reliability is the most important factor for JIT businesses and those in time-sensitive sectors, but for cost-leaders, luxury brands, and ethically positioned businesses, price, quality, and sustainability credentials respectively take precedence. The most significant factor that needs to be considered is the business’s operational strategy and inventory model, as these outweigh sector and market factors in determining which supplier attribute is paramount. Therefore, the claim that reliability is universally most important cannot be sustained — it is most important for JIT-dependent manufacturers, but significantly less so for businesses with alternative inventory strategies or differentiated competitive positions.

Analyse: Labour Intensive vs Capital Intensive [9 marks] — 2022 P1 Q21

Two Exemplar Paragraphs (7–8 Chains of Analysis Each)

Paragraph 1 — Labour Intensive Allows Flexibility and Customisation

One reason why a business might choose labour intensive production is that it enables greater flexibility and customisation in meeting individual customer requirements. This means that in markets where consumers demand bespoke or personalised products — such as high-end furniture, specialist legal services, or artisan food production — workers can adapt their skills, judgement and craft to each unique commission in ways that automated machinery cannot replicate. Therefore, a labour intensive approach enables the business to compete on product uniqueness and customer experience rather than price, as a result creating significant differentiation from capital-intensive competitors who are locked into standardised mass production runs (Porter’s Generic Strategies). This could lead to a premium pricing strategy becoming viable, as consumers perceive greater added value in hand-crafted or personalised products than in machine-produced equivalents, causing revenue per unit to rise substantially even where output volume remains lower than capital-intensive alternatives. Furthermore, labour intensive businesses do not incur the significant capital expenditure associated with automated production lines, meaning lower financial risk during periods of demand volatility — workers can be redeployed or, in extremis, made redundant at lower sunk cost than writing off expensive machinery. This thus leads to the conclusion that labour intensive production is strategically appropriate for businesses pursuing differentiation strategies in niche markets where customisation, craftsmanship, and customer responsiveness are the primary sources of competitive advantage, and where the premium pricing enabled by these qualities generates profit margins that more than compensate for higher unit labour costs.

Paragraph 2 — Labour Intensive Is Appropriate in Low-Wage Economies

A further reason why a business might choose labour intensive production is that in economies where wage rates are significantly lower than capital equipment costs, labour intensive methods deliver lower unit costs than automation. This means that a manufacturing business offshoring production to South-East Asia or Sub-Saharan Africa, for example, may find that employing large numbers of workers at local wage rates produces each unit far more cheaply than investing in robotic production lines, therefore achieving the cost-leadership competitive position (Porter’s Generic Strategies) that drives mass-market profitability without requiring the capital outlay and ongoing maintenance expenditure of automated alternatives. As a result, the business retains financial flexibility to invest surplus capital in marketing, product development, or market development strategies (Ansoff’s Matrix) that drive revenue growth, rather than tying capital up in depreciating plant and machinery. This could lead to a more agile business model that can scale production volumes rapidly by hiring additional workers in response to demand increases, rather than waiting months for new capital equipment to be procured, installed and commissioned. Furthermore, many governments in developing economies offer tax incentives and subsidised infrastructure to labour-intensive foreign investors to drive employment creation, causing effective unit costs to fall further still as fiscal benefits are captured alongside the fundamental wage cost advantage. This thus leads to the conclusion that labour intensive production represents the profit-maximising choice in low-wage markets for standardised goods, provided that adequate quality management systems ensure that lower wage costs do not translate into product defects that would trigger returns, regulatory action, or reputational damage that erodes the cost advantage achieved.

Analyse: How Speed-e-Puds Benefits from High Capacity Utilisation [12 marks] — 2024 P3 Q1

Two Exemplar Paragraphs (7–8 Chains of Analysis Each)

Paragraph 1 — Lower Unit Costs Through Fixed Cost Spreading

One benefit of high capacity utilisation for Speed-e-Puds is the significant reduction in unit costs achieved by spreading fixed costs across a greater volume of output. This means that Speed-e-Puds’s factory overheads — rent, equipment depreciation, management salaries and utility standing charges — remain constant regardless of output volume, therefore the more units of desserts produced within a given period, the lower the fixed cost allocated to each individual unit. As a result, if the factory has capacity for 10,000 desserts per day with fixed costs of £5,000 daily, operating at 90% utilisation (9,000 units) yields a fixed cost per unit of £0.56, compared to £1.00 at 50% utilisation — a 44% cost reduction purely from higher output volume. This could lead to Speed-e-Puds achieving a cost-leadership position (Porter’s Generic Strategies) in the dessert supply market, enabling it to offer competitive prices to supermarket buyers while maintaining healthy profit margins that fund ongoing product development and potential market development into new retail channels (Ansoff’s Matrix). Furthermore, lower unit costs allow Speed-e-Puds to absorb promotional pricing pressures from major supermarket buyers who regularly seek price concessions from suppliers, causing the business to retain profitable supply contracts that smaller, less efficient competitors cannot sustain at the same price point. This thus leads to the conclusion that high capacity utilisation provides Speed-e-Puds with a structural cost advantage that supports both its competitive pricing strategy and its ability to win and retain major retail contracts in an intensely competitive chilled dessert market.

Paragraph 2 — Improved Return on Capital and Investment Credibility

A further benefit for Speed-e-Puds is that high capacity utilisation maximises the return generated from its significant investment in factory equipment and production infrastructure. This means that the business’s non-current asset base — which likely represents the largest single item on its balance sheet — generates the maximum possible revenue output when operated at or near full capacity, therefore improving Speed-e-Puds’s return on capital employed (ROCE), a key metric scrutinised by lenders and equity investors when assessing management effectiveness and operational efficiency. As a result, strong ROCE signals to external stakeholders that Speed-e-Puds’s capital is being deployed productively, enhancing the business’s credibility when seeking additional financing for capacity expansion or product innovation investment. This could lead to a virtuous cycle: high utilisation → strong ROCE → lower cost of capital → affordable investment in additional capacity → economies of scale through higher output → further unit cost reductions and competitive strengthening. Furthermore, for a business supplying major supermarket chains, demonstrated operational efficiency and factory utilisation data provide commercial negotiation leverage — evidence that Speed-e-Puds can fulfil large volume orders reliably and at competitive cost, causing supermarket buyers to prefer Speed-e-Puds as a primary supplier over smaller competitors whose lower utilisation signals higher unit costs and potential fulfilment risk. This thus leads to the conclusion that high capacity utilisation provides Speed-e-Puds with both internal financial strength and external commercial credibility that compound each other to support long-term competitive advantage in the chilled dessert supply market.

2017 P1 Q3 — Spare Capacity Benefits

A business operates at 55% capacity utilisation. Which statement about this is correct?

A. There is more time available for equipment maintenance ✔
B. Unit costs will be lower than at higher utilisation
C. The business can meet unexpected increases in demand less easily
D. Workers are likely to be under greater pressure

Correct Answer: A

Why this is correct: Lower capacity utilisation = more downtime available = more time for planned maintenance without disrupting production. B is wrong because lower utilisation = HIGHER unit costs (fixed costs spread over fewer units). C is wrong because spare capacity makes it EASIER to meet unexpected demand. D is wrong because lower utilisation means less pressure, not more.

Examiner Trap
This is the foundational MCQ trap for Unit 3.4. AQA retested the identical principle in 2020 P1 Q3. Students who believe “higher utilisation is always better” get these wrong. Lower utilisation has genuine operational benefits — maintenance windows, demand flexibility, worker wellbeing.
Strategy Tip
The optimal utilisation rate is around 85–90%: low enough for maintenance and flexibility, high enough to keep unit costs competitive. This nuance is what separates a top-grade answer from an average one.

2019 P1 Q3 — Capital Intensive & Unit Costs

A business becomes more capital intensive, increasing capacity by 10% with output also rising 10%. Unit costs rise by 5%. Which is true?

A. Economies of scale are occurring
B. Diseconomies of scale are occurring ✔
C. Labour productivity is falling
D. Capacity utilisation has fallen

Correct Answer: B

Why this is correct: If output rises 10% but unit costs also rise 5%, the business is experiencing diseconomies of scale — costs are rising faster in proportion to benefits. More capital intensive = machinery replacing workers, which increases depreciation and maintenance costs that outweigh the savings. Capacity utilisation is unchanged (output and capacity both rose 10%). Labour productivity may have risen (fewer workers, same output).

Examiner Trap
Students assume “more capital intensive = lower unit costs.” Not always. If machinery costs (purchase, depreciation, maintenance) exceed the labour cost saved, unit costs RISE. This is diseconomies of scale from overcapitalisation.
Strategy Tip
Always check UNIT COSTS when evaluating capital investment. If unit costs rise despite higher output, diseconomies of scale are occurring — regardless of how much capital was invested.

2019 P1 Q5 — Comparing Factory Data

Factory X: Output 800, Employees 40, Leavers 4. Factory Y: Output 1000, Employees 60, Leavers 9. Which statement is TRUE?

A. Factory X has higher labour retention AND higher labour productivity ✔
B. Factory X has higher labour retention but lower labour productivity
C. Factory Y has higher labour retention AND higher labour productivity
D. Factory Y has higher labour retention but lower labour productivity

Correct Answer: A

Full workings:
Labour productivity: X = 800/40 = 20 units/employee. Y = 1000/60 = 16.7 units/employee. Factory X is higher. ✓
Labour turnover: X = 4/40 = 10% turnover → retention = 90%. Y = 9/60 = 15% turnover → retention = 85%. Factory X has higher retention. ✓

Examiner Trap
Students may confuse labour turnover with retention, or forget to calculate all metrics before answering. Always calculate ALL required figures explicitly — do not estimate or guess from absolute numbers alone.

2020 P1 Q3 — Capacity Utilisation Effects

S1: An increase in capacity utilisation allows more time for equipment maintenance. S2: An increase in capacity utilisation makes it easier to meet unexpected increases in demand.

A. S1 true, S2 true
B. S1 true, S2 false
C. S1 false, S2 true
D. S1 false, S2 false ✔

Correct Answer: D — Both false

Why this is correct: HIGHER utilisation = LESS downtime = LESS time for maintenance (S1 FALSE). HIGHER utilisation = LESS spare capacity = HARDER to meet unexpected demand spikes (S2 FALSE). Both benefits belong to LOWER utilisation, not higher. This is arguably AQA’s most frequently retested MCQ principle in Unit 3.4.

Examiner Trap
This exact principle was tested in 2017 AND 2020. If you see “higher capacity utilisation allows...” statements about flexibility or maintenance, the answer is almost always FALSE.

2020 P1 Q5 — Lean Production & Bulk Buying

S1: Lean production reduces unit costs by buying raw materials in greater bulk. S2: Lean production reduces unit costs by reducing wastage of raw materials.

A. S1 true, S2 true
B. S1 true, S2 false
C. S1 false, S2 true ✔
D. S1 false, S2 false

Correct Answer: C

Why this is correct: Lean / JIT does the OPPOSITE of bulk buying — it uses small, frequent deliveries to minimise stock held (S1 FALSE). Lean absolutely does reduce unit costs through waste elimination (Muda) — eliminating overproduction, defects, excess inventory, and unnecessary motion (S2 TRUE).

Examiner Trap
“Bulk buying” is a JIC strategy, NOT a lean/JIT strategy. Lean production and bulk purchasing are conceptually opposite approaches. Never confuse them.
Strategy Tip
Lean production's 7 wastes (Muda): overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, defects. Knowing these gives you endless analysis material for essay questions.

2022 P1 Q6 — Employee Costs as % of Turnover

Which change would MOST likely increase employee costs as a percentage of turnover?

A. An increase in the number of employees
B. An increase in revenue per employee
C. A fall in the prices charged to customers ✔
D. An increase in labour productivity

Correct Answer: C

Why this is correct: Employee costs as % of turnover = (employee costs ÷ turnover) × 100. If PRICES FALL, TURNOVER FALLS. If employee costs stay constant but the denominator (turnover) shrinks, the percentage RISES. Students who only think about increasing the numerator (employee costs) miss that decreasing the denominator achieves the same effect — and often more powerfully.

Examiner Trap
Always think about BOTH the numerator AND denominator of this ratio. The percentage can rise because employee costs increase OR because turnover falls. A is wrong because more employees = higher numerator but also potentially higher output/turnover, which could offset. C is the clearest, most direct mechanism.

2022 P1 Q10 — Quality & PED

S1: Improving quality is likely to make demand more price elastic. S2: Improving quality is likely to increase added value.

A. S1 true, S2 true
B. S1 true, S2 false
C. S1 false, S2 true ✔
D. S1 false, S2 false

Correct Answer: C

Why this is correct: Better quality creates brand loyalty and differentiation → customers are LESS likely to switch on price → demand becomes more price INELASTIC (S1 FALSE). Improving quality allows higher prices relative to input costs → added value = selling price − bought-in costs → RISES (S2 TRUE).

Examiner Trap
Quality → inelastic demand (NOT elastic). The logic: quality differentiates, differentiation reduces substitutability, reduced substitutability makes consumers less price-sensitive. This chain is tested repeatedly. AQA retested in 2024 P1 Q11.

2023 P1 Q1 — What Causes Spare Capacity to RISE?

Which of the following would most likely cause spare capacity to RISE?

A. New competitors entering the market ✔
B. A successful advertising campaign
C. A reduction in the price charged to customers
D. Opening a new distribution channel

Correct Answer: A

Why this is correct: New competitors enter → market share is diluted → demand for YOUR products falls → you produce less relative to your capacity → spare capacity RISES. All other options increase demand for the business’s own products, which would REDUCE spare capacity, not increase it.

Examiner Trap
The question asks what causes spare capacity to RISE (not fall). Spare capacity is the GAP between actual output and maximum capacity. Anything that reduces actual output (demand falls) or increases maximum capacity (new factory) without matching demand increase raises spare capacity.

2024 P1 Q10 — Unit Costs, Scale & Utilisation

Data shows output rose significantly from 2020–2021 while unit costs fell. Which explanations are TRUE?

S1: Economies of scale occurred — TRUE
S2: Higher capacity utilisation led to lower unit costs — TRUE (if capacity unchanged)

Answer: A (both true)

Why this is correct: If output rises and unit costs fall, fixed costs are being spread over more units = economies of scale. If capacity stays the same but output rises, capacity utilisation increases, which also directly reduces fixed cost per unit. Both mechanisms explain falling unit costs. The key check: if capacity also rose proportionally, utilisation is unchanged — but if only output rose, both explanations apply.

Examiner Trap
Verify whether capacity changed. If capacity doubled alongside output, utilisation is unchanged and S2 is false. If capacity stayed the same while output rose, both S1 and S2 are true.

2024 P1 Q11 — Quality, Inelasticity & Competitive Advantage

S1: Improving quality is likely to make demand more price inelastic. S2: Improving quality is likely to lead to competitive advantage.

A. S1 true, S2 true ✔
B. S1 true, S2 false
C. S1 false, S2 true
D. S1 false, S2 false

Correct Answer: A — Both true

Why this is correct: Quality → brand loyalty → lower substitutability → price inelastic demand (S1 TRUE). Quality differentiates from competitors → competitive advantage through differentiation strategy (S2 TRUE). This is essentially the same principle retested from 2022 P1 Q10 with reversed framing.

Strategy Tip
Memorise the chain: Quality improvement → brand loyalty → inelastic demand → premium pricing possible → added value rises → competitive advantage. This single chain answers multiple MCQs and earns analysis marks in every quality essay.

2025 P1 Q13 — Targets vs Actuals

QAQ plc data shows actual performance vs targets for four metrics. Which metric has QAQ FAILED to meet its target?

Approach: Calculate or compare each metric with its target. Identify the one where actual < target (for “at least” targets) or actual > target (for “no more than” targets).

Key technique: Read the direction of the target. “At least X%” means actual must be ≥X. “No more than X%” means actual must be ≤X. Labour turnover targets are “no more than” (you want it LOW). ROCE and market share targets are “at least” (you want them HIGH).

Examiner Trap
The direction of “better” differs by metric. Higher ROCE = better. LOWER labour turnover = better. Read target language carefully (“at least” vs “no more than”) before comparing actual to target.

2018 P1 Q15 — Re-Order Level Calculation

Buffer stock = 250 units. Usage rate = 50 units per day. Lead time = 5 days. What is the re-order level?

A. 50 units
B. 250 units
C. 300 units
D. 500 units ✔

Correct Answer: D — 500 units

Full working: Re-order level = (Lead time × Daily usage) + Buffer stock = (5 × 50) + 250 = 250 + 250 = 500 units.

Examiner Trap
The most common error is forgetting to ADD the buffer stock: students calculate (5 × 50) = 250 and stop there. The buffer exists precisely so stock doesn’t hit zero during lead time. The re-order level must be set high enough that even after lead time usage, you still have your buffer remaining.

2019 P1 Q11 — Incorrect Order Quantity from Chart

An inventory chart shows one delivery bringing stock ABOVE the maximum level. On which day was the incorrect quantity ordered?

Answer: Identify the day where the vertical delivery jump exceeds the maximum stock line.

Why this is correct: Normal deliveries bring stock exactly back to the maximum level. If one delivery pushes stock above the maximum line, too many units were ordered on that occasion. Read the x-axis day value at the point of the anomalous vertical jump.

Examiner Trap
Only ONE delivery in the chart will be abnormal. Do not assume the first, last, or most recent — check all vertical jumps and identify which one exceeds the maximum stock level.

Employee Costs as % of Turnover — 2020 P1 Q17 (Full Worked Example)

Inventory Control Calculations — 2022 P2 Q3 (DT Case Study)

Reading from the Chart

Lead time: Find where stock hits re-order level (order placed) → find where stock jumps vertically (delivery arrives) → count days between = lead time.

Re-order quantity: Read the SIZE of the vertical jump = top of jump − bottom of jump.

Daily usage rate: Pick two points on the declining line → usage = (stock at point A − stock at point B) ÷ (number of days between A and B).

Buffer stock: Read the lowest level stock reaches just before delivery arrives (the bottom of each “sawtooth”).

Calculation Exam Technique for Unit 3.4
1. Always write the FORMULA before substituting numbers — this earns the method mark.
2. Check capacity utilisation — if given, you MUST calculate actual output, not use maximum capacity.
3. Watch for “per day” vs “per week” vs “per year” — match time periods throughout.
4. On inventory charts: use a ruler to read values precisely. Approximate readings lose marks.
5. Show units in your answer — “44.4%” not just “44.4”. “£0.56 per unit” not just “0.56”.
6. Employee costs as % of turnover: remember BOTH the numerator and denominator can change. Don’t assume only one moves.
Summary Unit 3.4 — Final Exam Reminders & Judgement Phrases
The 12 Non-Negotiable Rules for Unit 3.4
1. Capacity utilisation = (actual output ÷ max capacity) × 100. High utilisation lowers unit costs but reduces flexibility. The OPTIMAL range is ~85–90%, not 100%.
2. Higher capacity utilisation does NOT mean more time for maintenance. It means LESS. This MCQ trap appears every 2–3 years.
3. Lean production reduces costs by ELIMINATING WASTE — not by bulk buying. JIT = small, frequent deliveries.
4. JIT requires reliable suppliers. COVID-19 and the semiconductor shortage proved JIT’s vulnerability. Use these examples in every lean production essay.
5. Improving quality makes demand more price INELASTIC (not elastic). Better quality = customer loyalty = less price sensitivity. This is tested every 2 years.
6. QA = prevent defects (everyone’s job). QC = detect defects (inspector’s job). QA is generally superior but more expensive to implement. In regulated industries, BOTH are needed.
7. Re-order level = (Lead time × Daily usage) + Buffer stock. Always ADD the buffer.
8. On inventory charts: lead time = gap between order and delivery. Re-order quantity = size of vertical jump. Usage rate = slope of declining line.
9. Employee costs as % of turnover can rise if revenue FALLS (even if employee costs don’t change). The denominator matters as much as the numerator.
10. Labour intensive ≠ always cheaper. Capital intensive ≠ always more efficient. The optimal mix depends on product type, wage levels, volume, and flexibility needs.
11. Supplier choice: reliability matters most for JIT businesses; price matters most for cost-leader businesses; quality matters most for premium brands. ALWAYS link to business context.
12. In “all businesses” evaluation questions, contrast manufacturers vs service businesses, JIT vs JIC users, large vs small, high-value vs low-value products.
