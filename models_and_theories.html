How to use models in exams

The rules that matter

1. Models must drive your argument

If removing the model's name doesn't weaken the paragraph, it was decoration.

Weak

"This links to Ansoff because the business enters a new market."

Strong

"Ansoff classifies this as market development — existing products in unfamiliar markets — which carries medium risk because the business lacks local knowledge, increasing marketing costs and slowing revenue growth."

2. Weave, don't dump

June 2026 examiners confirmed: models woven into chains earn marks; models described in isolation do not. Jasneet scored 9/9 by weaving Maslow → Taylor → Handy into one flowing chain.

Model dumping

"Ansoff has four strategies: penetration, product development, market development, diversification." This earns 1 mark and wastes time.

3. Use the relevant part only

Don't describe all four Ansoff quadrants or all five Porter forces. Pick what matters.

4. Only use models when they add value

A good argument with no model beats a forced model reference.

5. Pair models with real businesses

"Amazon's AWS represents diversification" is stronger than "a business pursuing diversification faces high risk."

6. Use models to evaluate, not just analyse

Ansoff's risk framework can qualify a conclusion. Porter's "stuck in the middle" can challenge a recommendation.

Ask yourself

Before each paragraph: "Is there a model that would make this argument stronger?" If yes, weave it in. If no, write without one.

Exam technique

How to weave a model into a paragraph

StepWhat you doExample
1. PointState the argument"International expansion could increase revenue."
2. ClassifyName the relevant part of the model"Ansoff classifies this as market development."
3. ExplainUse the model to deepen analysis"This carries medium risk — the business lacks local knowledge."
4. ChainDevelop consequences"Marketing costs will be higher, reducing margins for 2–3 years."
5. Link backConnect to the question"Therefore, while expansion offers growth, the risk means it isn't always the best direction."

  • Plots brands on two axes. A gap doesn't automatically mean demand exists.

  • Usage rate, reorder level, buffer stock, lead time. Poor control → cash flow and customer service problems.

Common mistakes

Defining without applying

Describe the quadrant, then explain why, then analyse consequences.

Confusing the quadrants

Market development = existing product, new market. Diversification = new product AND new market. Check both axes.

"Stuck in the middle"

From real student work

Exemplar paragraphs

Maslow woven into a 9-mark answer (Jasneet's 9/9, June 2026)

Maslow driving the chain

According to Maslow, physiological needs must be satisfied first — competitive pay addresses this, consistent with Taylor. Once met, safety needs become the focus — permanent contracts reduce the anxiety driving turnover. Beyond this, social needs matter — team working and what Handy calls task culture create belonging. Therefore, understanding the hierarchy helps target retention at the specific unsatisfied level.

Three models woven into one chain. No model described in isolation.

Ansoff unprompted in a branding essay

Branding enables strategic growth

A strong brand reduces the risk of what Ansoff classifies as market development. When Apple launched in China, its brand transferred trust to a new market, reducing marketing costs. This means branded businesses can pursue more ambitious growth at lower cost — a competitive advantage over weaker-branded rivals.
