3.7.1 Mission, Corporate Objectives, Strategy & SWOT Analysis

The Strategic Hierarchy

Diagram: From Mission to Tactics — The Strategic Hierarchy
Key Definitions
Mission statement = A qualitative statement of the business's purpose, values, and overarching reason for existing. Sets direction but is NOT measurable.
Corporate objective = A quantifiable goal for the whole business, derived from the mission (e.g., "Achieve 15% ROCE within 3 years").
Strategy = A medium- to long-term plan to achieve corporate objectives (e.g., "Enter the Chinese market through a joint venture").
Tactic = A short-term action to implement a strategy (e.g., "Run a promotional campaign in Q1").
Functional objectives = Targets set for each department (marketing, finance, HR, operations) that support corporate objectives.
Examiner Trap #1 — The Ranking MCQ (2025 P1 Q15)
AQA asks: "Which option correctly ranks from long-term to short-term?" The answer is Mission statement → Corporate objectives → Strategies → Tactics. The trap: students confuse the ORDER. Mission comes BEFORE objectives (mission inspires objectives). Objectives come BEFORE strategies (objectives say WHAT, strategies say HOW). Tactics are the most short-term. Option B in 2025 ("Corporate objectives → strategies → tactics") OMITS the mission and starts at objectives — this is also a correct PARTIAL sequence, but the full correct answer INCLUDES the mission at the top.

SWOT Analysis

Diagram: SWOT Analysis Framework
Examiner Trap #2 — Internal vs External Classification (2022 P1 Q11, 2024 P1 Q5, 2018 P1 Q2)
This is AQA's most repeated SWOT trap. The rule: INTERNAL = things the business controls (strengths/weaknesses). EXTERNAL = things outside the business's control (opportunities/threats).

2024 P1 Q5: "An increase in the number of patents held by a business" = STRENGTH (internal — the business chose to patent). NOT an opportunity.
2022 P1 Q11: "Improvements in infrastructure" = OPPORTUNITY (external — government built it, not the business). NOT a strength. "Increase in minimum wage" = THREAT (external legislation). NOT a weakness.
2018 P1 Q2: "Liquidation of a competitor" = OPPORTUNITY (external event). "Inefficient quality assurance" = WEAKNESS (internal failing). "New legislation requiring product modification" = THREAT (external). "Lower unemployment increasing demand" = OPPORTUNITY (external).

The golden rule: If the BUSINESS did it → internal. If the ENVIRONMENT changed → external.
Real-World Examples for SWOT
Apple (Strength): Brand loyalty, ecosystem lock-in, $162bn cash reserves, patents on key technologies.
Debenhams (Weakness): Outdated stores, high debt, failure to invest in e-commerce — went into administration 2020.
Netflix (Opportunity): COVID-19 lockdowns drove global demand for streaming — subscriber base surged by 37m in 2020.
UK Retailers (Threat): Rising minimum wage (from £9.50 to £11.44 by 2024) increased costs for labour-intensive retail businesses.
3.7.2 Financial Ratio Analysis — ROCE, Current Ratio, Gearing & Efficiency

The Five Ratios AQA Tests

Formulae You MUST Know — Every Single One is Tested
ROCE (%) = (Operating profit ÷ Capital employed) × 100
Capital employed = Total equity + Non-current liabilities (OR Total assets − Current liabilities)

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities
Expressed as a ratio, e.g., 2.1:1. Ideal is around 1.5–2:1

Gearing (%) = (Non-current liabilities ÷ Capital employed) × 100
Above 50% = highly geared. More reliant on debt = more risky.

Payables days = (Payables ÷ Cost of sales) × 365
How many days on average it takes to pay suppliers

Receivables days = (Receivables ÷ Revenue) × 365
How many days on average it takes to collect from customers

Inventory turnover (times) = Cost of sales ÷ Average inventory
How many times inventory is sold and replaced per year. Higher = more efficient.

What Each Ratio Tells You — And What Examiners Want

RatioWhat It MeasuresGood PerformanceExaminer Expects You to Say
ROCEHow efficiently the business generates profit from its capitalHigher than interest rate on borrowing; higher than competitors; rising over timeCompare to industry / previous year / cost of capital. Link to strategy decisions.
Current ratioAbility to pay short-term debts1.5–2:1. Below 1 = potential liquidity crisis. Too high = inefficient (cash tied up)Don't just calculate — say whether it's healthy. Explain implications for the business.
GearingProportion of long-term funding that is debtBelow 50%. High gearing = higher risk if interest rates rise, but also = tax-efficient leverageLink to interest rate sensitivity. Highly geared + rising interest rates = profit squeeze.
Payables daysHow long the business takes to pay suppliersLonger = better for cash flow, BUT may damage supplier relationshipsRising payables days could signal cash flow problems OR smart cash management.
Receivables daysHow long customers take to payLower = better for cash flowRising receivables = cash tied up. Compare to payables days for full picture.
Inventory turnoverHow quickly stock is soldHigher = more efficient (less cash tied up in stock)Low turnover could mean overstocking, slow sales, or obsolete inventory.
Examiner Trap #3 — Gearing Formula Errors (2017 P1 Q4, 2019 P1 Q17)
Gearing = Non-current liabilities ÷ Capital employed × 100. Capital employed = Total equity + Non-current liabilities. The most common error: students use TOTAL liabilities (including current liabilities) instead of NON-CURRENT liabilities only. Another trap: AQA may give you the gearing ratio and ask you to WORK BACKWARDS to find one of the missing values. In 2019 P1 Q17, gearing was 40% with NCL of £200,000. Students had to find CE (£200,000 ÷ 0.4 = £500,000), then recalculate after adding £100,000 loan (new NCL = £300,000, new CE = £600,000, new gearing = 50%).
Examiner Trap #4 — Current Ratio After Transactions (2023 P1 Q17)
AQA loves giving an initial balance sheet then describing transactions that change current assets and liabilities. You must adjust EACH item individually:
2023: Wot plc receives £20m from shares. Uses £15m on machinery (non-current asset), £3m to pay overdraft (reduces current liabilities), £2m to increase inventory (increases current assets).
New current assets = 31 − 3 (overdraft was in CA?) Actually: original CA = £31m, add £2m inventory = £33m. But £3m overdraft payoff reduces CL. Wait — overdraft is a current LIABILITY. So new CL = 18 − 3 = £15m. New CA = 31 + 2 = £33m. No — the share money (£20m) goes through cash first. Cash +20, then cash −15 (machinery), cash −3 (overdraft), cash −2 (inventory). Net cash change = 0. But inventory rises by 2. So CA = 31 + 2 = 33? No — the remaining unspent cash also stays. Let me recalculate: Original CA = 31. Add 20m cash from shares = 51. Spend 15m on machinery = 36 (machinery is NCA, not CA). Pay 3m overdraft = 33 (overdraft in CL reduces, cash reduces). Buy 2m inventory = 33 (cash −2, inventory +2, net CA unchanged). Final CA = 33. CL = 18 − 3 = 15. Current ratio = 33 ÷ 15 = 2.2:1.
The trap: Not tracking each transaction's impact on BOTH sides of the balance sheet separately.
Examiner Trap #5 — Low Current Ratio + High/Low Gearing (2024 P1 Q13, 2018 P1 Q7)
2024: "Current liabilities are 3× current assets" → Current ratio = CA ÷ CL. If CL = 3 × CA, then ratio = CA ÷ 3CA = 0.33:1 = LOW current ratio. "Shareholders provide 20% of CE" → Gearing = NCL ÷ CE. If equity = 20% of CE, then NCL = 80% of CE → Gearing = 80% = HIGH gearing. Answer: C — low current ratio and high gearing.
2018: "More current liabilities than current assets" → ratio below 1 = LOW. "Shareholders provide 90% of CE" → NCL = 10% of CE → Gearing = 10% = LOW. Answer: D — low current ratio and low gearing.
Students must read BOTH conditions separately, not assume high/low for both.
Gross Profit vs Operating Profit — Know the Difference (2025 P1 Q7)
Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales. Bromley Apples: £20m − £6m = £14m. Answer: C.
Operating profit = Gross profit − Operating expenses. So operating profit = £14m − £4m = £10m.
Profit for the year = Operating profit − Tax. So profit for year = £10m − £7m = £3m.
The trap: students subtract ALL costs from revenue to get £3m — that's profit for the year, NOT gross profit. Read the question: it asks for GROSS profit only.
How 3.7.2 Financial Ratios Comes Up in the Exam — What to Expect & What to Do
Paper 1 MCQs (1 mark): Income statement understanding (gross vs operating vs profit for year) tested 2020, 2023, 2025. Budget variance MCQs tested 2018, 2024. Gearing/current ratio interpretation tested 2017, 2018, 2024. Action: learn the income statement structure cold. Gross profit = Revenue − COS. Operating profit = Gross profit − expenses. Rent is an expense (does NOT affect gross profit). Tax reduces profit for the year, not operating profit.

Paper 1 Calculations (2–4 marks): Ratio calculations appear EVERY year without fail. Current ratio (2022, 2023, 2025). Gearing (2019). ROCE (2025). Payables/receivables days (2022). Budget variance (2020). Action: show ALL working. State the formula first. Round to 2dp. State whether the result is healthy/concerning.

Paper 1 Analyse (9 marks): Receivables/payables changes → cash (2020). Share capital vs bank loan (2024). Debt → dividends (2019). Action: always link the ratio change to a BUSINESS CONSEQUENCE — don’t just describe the numbers.

Paper 1 Essays (25 marks): Financial ratios still important? (2019). Ratios for non-profits? (2024). Action: argue ratios are necessary but not sufficient. For non-profits, combine with mission-specific outcome measures.

Paper 2 Calculations: Payables days (2024). Current ratio + gearing (2020). ROCE (2023). M&S full ratio set (2018). Action: use CASE STUDY data. Don’t just calculate — interpret what the number means for THIS business.
3.7.3 Overall Performance — Core Competences & Elkington's Triple Bottom Line

Assessing Overall Performance Beyond Financial Data

AQA expects students to understand that financial ratios alone give an incomplete picture. Businesses should also analyse operations data (capacity utilisation, quality, inventory), HR data (turnover, productivity, absenteeism), and marketing data (market share, customer satisfaction, brand strength).

Core Competences
Definition: The unique combination of resources, skills, knowledge, and capabilities that give a business a competitive advantage and are difficult for rivals to replicate.
Characteristics: (1) Provide access to many markets. (2) Contribute significantly to customer-perceived benefits. (3) Difficult for competitors to imitate.
Example: Apple's core competence is design integration — combining hardware, software, and services into a seamless ecosystem. Competitors can copy individual products but not the ecosystem effect.

Elkington's Triple Bottom Line (TBL)

Diagram: Elkington's Triple Bottom Line
Examiner Trap #6 — "Profit Is a Financial Target for ALL Businesses" (2023 P1 Q10)
The statement "Profit is a financial target for all businesses" is FALSE. Non-profit organisations (charities, mutuals, social enterprises) do NOT have profit as their primary financial target. They may aim for a surplus to reinvest, but profit maximisation is not their goal. The other options (profit is a measure in Elkington's TBL, profit is a reward for risk, profit is a source of finance) are all TRUE. Students who don't consider non-profits pick the wrong answer.
Examiner Trap #7 — TBL "Will Lead to Lower Profits" (2017 P1 Q23)
The 25-mark essay asks whether adopting Elkington's TBL will lead to lower profits. The trap: writing a one-sided answer. The top band response argues BOTH: (1) Yes — environmental and social measures increase costs (sustainable materials, higher wages, community investment). (2) No — TBL can INCREASE profits long-term through stronger brand reputation, customer loyalty, reduced regulatory risk, and attracting talented employees. The judgement: short-term costs may rise, but long-term profitability can improve. It depends on the industry, the business's competitive position, and consumer attitudes.

Examiner Trap #8 — Fiscal vs Monetary Classification (2019 P1 Q9)
"Decreasing government subsidies on agriculture" — students must identify: subsidies are government spending = FISCAL policy. Reducing subsidies removes protection for domestic producers = encourages open trade (cheaper imports can compete). Answer: A — Fiscal policy which encourages more open trade. The trap: students who think subsidies involve interest rates pick monetary policy. ANY government taxation or spending decision = fiscal. Only interest rates = monetary.

Exchange Rates — The Chain of Effects

Exchange Rate Rules — Learn This Chain Cold
£ RISES (appreciation):
→ UK exports become MORE expensive abroad → export demand FALLS
→ Imports become CHEAPER for UK buyers → import demand RISES
→ UK manufacturers face more competition from cheap imports
→ BUT raw materials imported become cheaper → lower production costs

£ FALLS (depreciation):
→ UK exports become CHEAPER abroad → export demand RISES
→ Imports become MORE expensive → import demand FALLS
→ UK manufacturers more competitive domestically and abroad
→ BUT imported raw materials cost more → higher production costs

Exchange Rate Worked Examples — UK Company Trading with the US

Scenario: BritParts Ltd (UK manufacturer) buys components from AmeriSupply Inc (US supplier)
BritParts Ltd imports $100,000 worth of components from the US each month. It also exports £50,000 worth of finished products to US customers. The exchange rate changes from £1 = $1.40 to £1 = $1.20 (the pound has FALLEN / depreciated).
Before: £1 = $1.40After: £1 = $1.20Effect
IMPORTING: BritParts buys $100,000 of US components$100,000 ÷ 1.40 = £71,429$100,000 ÷ 1.20 = £83,333£11,904 MORE expensive. The weaker pound means each dollar costs more in sterling. Import costs RISE.
EXPORTING: BritParts sells £50,000 of products to US customers£50,000 × 1.40 = $70,000 to the US customer£50,000 × 1.20 = $60,000 to the US customer$10,000 CHEAPER for US buyer. UK exports become cheaper abroad → demand likely to rise.
Now the Reverse: The Pound RISES from £1 = $1.40 to £1 = $1.60
Before: £1 = $1.40After: £1 = $1.60Effect
IMPORTING: BritParts buys $100,000 of US components$100,000 ÷ 1.40 = £71,429$100,000 ÷ 1.60 = £62,500£8,929 CHEAPER. The stronger pound means each dollar costs less. Import costs FALL.
EXPORTING: BritParts sells £50,000 of products to US customers£50,000 × 1.40 = $70,000£50,000 × 1.60 = $80,000$10,000 MORE expensive for US buyer. UK exports become dearer abroad → demand likely to fall.
The Memory Rule
£ RISES = imports CHEAPER, exports DEARER (good for importers, bad for exporters).
£ FALLS = imports DEARER, exports CHEAPER (bad for importers, good for exporters).

The calculation: For IMPORTS (buying foreign currency), DIVIDE the foreign price by the exchange rate. For EXPORTS (converting £ to foreign price), MULTIPLY the £ price by the exchange rate. A higher exchange rate means you get MORE foreign currency per pound — so imports cost LESS and exports cost MORE to the foreign buyer.
Examiner Trap #9 — Exchange Rate + GDP Combined (2025 P1 Q3)
"Which option leads to the LARGEST increase in UK imports?" Rising GDP means UK consumers have more income → buy more, including imports. Rising £ means imports are CHEAPER → UK buys more imports. So the answer is D — rise in GDP AND rise in exchange rate. Both forces push imports UP. The trap: students forget that GDP growth drives import demand (wealthier country buys more from abroad) and only consider the exchange rate effect.
Examiner Trap #10 — Interest Rates + Highly Geared Exporter (2024 P1 Q20)
This 9-mark analyse question combines TWO pressures. A significant UK interest rate rise affects a highly geared exporter in two ways: (1) Gearing effect: High debt → large interest payments → interest costs rise significantly → operating profit squeezed → potential cash flow crisis. (2) Export effect: Higher interest rates attract foreign investment → £ appreciates → exports become more expensive → demand for exports falls → revenue falls. Students who only discuss ONE effect cap at mid-band. The best answer discusses BOTH and links them: falling revenue + rising costs = severe profit squeeze.

Examiner Trap #12 — Porter's Forces and Profitability Direction (2018 P1 Q9, 2025 P1 Q14)
2018: "Which event is MOST LIKELY to HELP profitability?" A decrease in supplier power = suppliers have less leverage = business can negotiate cheaper inputs = lower costs = higher profit margins. The trap: students pick "new product development by competitor" — this is a THREAT (increases rivalry), not a help. Always ask: does this force go UP or DOWN, and what does that mean for MY costs or MY prices?

2025: "Low supplier power may encourage a retailer to use cost leadership" = TRUE (cheap inputs enable low costs). "A market leader may use low prices to create a barrier to entry" = TRUE (predatory/limit pricing deters new entrants). Answer: A — Both true.
3.7.8 Investment Appraisal — Payback, ARR & NPV

The Three Methods — How to Calculate Each

Payback Period
What it measures: How long it takes for the initial investment to be recovered from net cash flows.
Formula: Count the years of cumulative cash flow until the initial investment is paid back. If payback falls during a year, interpolate: Payback = Year before full recovery + (Remaining amount ÷ Cash flow in recovery year).
Advantage: Simple, focuses on liquidity and risk (shorter = safer).
Limitation: Ignores cash flows AFTER payback; ignores time value of money; ignores total profitability.
Average Rate of Return (ARR)
Formula: ARR (%) = (Average annual profit ÷ Initial investment) × 100
Average annual profit = (Total net returns − Initial investment) ÷ Number of years
Advantage: Considers total profitability over the project's life; gives a percentage for easy comparison.
Limitation: Ignores timing of returns; ignores time value of money; uses averages (hides peaks/troughs).
Net Present Value (NPV)
Formula: NPV = Sum of (each year's net cash flow × that year's discount factor) − Initial investment
Or: NPV = Sum of all discounted cash flows (including the negative Year 0 outflow)
Decision rule: Positive NPV = invest (project earns more than the discount rate). Negative NPV = reject.
Advantage: Considers time value of money; accounts for ALL cash flows; most theoretically sound method.
Limitation: Complex; relies on accurate forecasts and choosing the right discount rate; difficult to explain to non-financial managers.
Examiner Trap #13 — ARR Calculation Errors (2020 P1 Q9)
Data: Year 0: (£1,000k). Year 1: £500k. Year 2: £900k. "After two years, the ARR (%) is..."
Step 1: Total net returns = £500k + £900k = £1,400k
Step 2: Total profit = £1,400k − £1,000k = £400k
Step 3: Average annual profit = £400k ÷ 2 = £200k
Step 4: ARR = (£200k ÷ £1,000k) × 100 = 20%. Answer: D.
The traps: (1) Forgetting to subtract the initial investment from total returns. (2) Using total profit instead of AVERAGE annual profit. (3) Dividing by the wrong number of years. (4) Answer option A (90%) comes from not subtracting the investment at all: 1400/1000 × 100 ÷ 2... but students get confused.
Examiner Trap #14 — NPV When Year 1 Is Negative (2023 P2 Q1.1)
AJS plc invests £500k in Year 0. Year 1 cash flow is NEGATIVE (−£50k). Students panic. But the method is identical: multiply each year's net cash flow by its discount factor, including negative ones.
Year 0: −500 × 1.00 = −500. Year 1: −50 × 0.91 = −45.5. Year 2: 250 × 0.83 = 207.5. Year 3: 600 × 0.75 = 450.
NPV = −500 + (−45.5) + 207.5 + 450 = £112,000 (positive). Project should proceed.
The trap: students add Year 1 instead of subtracting it, or skip Year 0 thinking the discount factor of 1.00 means "ignore."

Sensitivity Analysis

What It Is & Why AQA Tests It
Definition: A technique that examines how the predicted financial outcome of an investment changes when ONE variable is altered (e.g., sales volume, costs, exchange rate).
Purpose: Tests "what if?" scenarios. Identifies which variables the investment is most sensitive to. Helps managers assess RISK.
Limitation: Only changes one variable at a time (in reality, multiple factors change simultaneously). Still relies on estimates. Does not tell you the PROBABILITY of each scenario.
How 3.7.8 Investment Appraisal Comes Up in the Exam — What to Expect & What to Do
Paper 1 MCQs (1 mark): ARR calculation from data (2020 Q9). Sensitivity analysis definition (2017 Q7). Decision tree: net gain definition (2022 Q3), probability calculation (2024 Q9). Action: for ARR, always subtract the initial investment from total returns FIRST, then divide by years, then divide by investment. For decision trees, remember probabilities must sum to 1.0.

Paper 1 Calculations (3–4 marks): Payback (2017 Q16). NPV (2017 Q17, 2024 Q17). Action: show every step. For payback, identify the year where cumulative cash flow turns positive, then calculate the exact month. For NPV, multiply each year’s net return by discount factor, then sum all values including the negative Year 0.

Paper 1 Analyse (9 marks): Decision tree analysis — why Option A might not be best (2019 Q18). Action: calculate expected values for all options, then explain NON-FINANCIAL reasons why the highest EV might not be the best choice (risk, probability assumptions, qualitative factors).

Paper 2 Mixed (4–16 marks): NPV calculation (2023 Q1.1). Sensitivity analysis explanation (2020 Q1.2). Investment recommendation (2020 Q1.4). Decision tree for strategic choice (2025 Q1.3). Action: for recommendation questions, ALWAYS discuss BOTH the quantitative results (payback, ARR, NPV) AND qualitative factors (risk, strategic fit, opportunity cost, non-financial benefits).

Paper 3 Evaluate (16 marks): Calculate payback + ARR, then recommend (2019 P3 Q4). Action: calculate accurately first, then evaluate using the results alongside case study context. A positive NPV does not automatically mean “invest” — consider the business’s financial position, risk appetite, and alternative uses of the funds.

Essay: "To what extent is a SWOT analysis valuable for any business deciding on its future strategy?" [24 marks] — 2019 P3 Q6

Model Answer Guidance

Introduction

SWOT analysis is a strategic tool that categorises a business's internal strengths and weaknesses alongside external opportunities and threats. The question of its "value" requires assessment of both its usefulness in strategic decision-making and its limitations — and whether these vary by business type and context.

Argument FOR — Structured Overview of Strategic Position

SWOT provides managers with a structured framework to assess where the business currently stands. By systematically identifying internal capabilities (strengths such as a strong brand or skilled workforce) and weaknesses (such as high gearing or outdated technology), managers gain a clear picture of what the business CAN and CANNOT do. Combined with external analysis of opportunities (growing markets, favourable legislation) and threats (new competitors, economic downturn), SWOT produces a holistic snapshot that can inform strategic choices. For example, a business identifying a strength in innovation and an opportunity in growing demand for sustainable products may decide to pursue differentiation through green product development. Without SWOT, strategic decisions risk being based on intuition or incomplete information — leading to poor resource allocation. For a small business like Logger Boards Ltd (2023 P3), SWOT helped the owner Bodhi identify that his environmental reputation was both a strength and aligned with market opportunities, guiding his organic growth strategy.

Argument FOR — Identifies Mismatches and Risks

SWOT's value also lies in exposing dangerous mismatches. A business planning aggressive expansion (strategy) may discover through SWOT that it has a weakness in cash flow and a threat from rising interest rates — both of which make borrowing to fund expansion risky. This early warning function can prevent costly strategic errors. Similarly, SWOT can reveal where strengths align with opportunities (offensive strategy) or where weaknesses coincide with threats (defensive strategy urgently needed). This matching process is what makes SWOT analytically useful rather than just a descriptive list.

Counter-argument — Static and Subjective

However, SWOT has significant limitations. It is a STATIC snapshot — it captures the position at one point in time but markets change rapidly. A strength today (e.g., proprietary technology) can become a weakness tomorrow if competitors innovate faster. Furthermore, SWOT is inherently SUBJECTIVE — what one manager classifies as a strength, another may see differently. There is no weighting mechanism: a minor strength (good office location) sits alongside a critical one (market-leading patent) with no indication of relative importance. For a large multinational facing complex, fast-moving markets, this lack of depth and dynamism makes SWOT insufficient as a standalone strategic tool.

Counter-argument — Descriptive, Not Prescriptive

Critically, SWOT identifies WHAT the situation is — but not WHAT TO DO about it. It does not generate strategies; it merely provides a platform from which strategies must still be developed. A small business owner may complete a SWOT and still be unsure whether to focus on exploiting strengths or addressing weaknesses. More sophisticated tools — Porter's Five Forces for competitive analysis, investment appraisal for financial decisions, Ansoff's matrix for growth direction — provide more specific strategic guidance. SWOT works best as a STARTING POINT for deeper analysis, not as the final word.

Judgement — Weighted

SWOT analysis is a valuable but insufficient tool for strategic planning. Its greatest value lies in providing a structured starting point that forces managers to consider both internal and external factors — preventing the common error of only looking inward. For small businesses with limited resources, SWOT's simplicity is an advantage: it requires no specialist knowledge and can be completed quickly. However, for large, complex businesses facing dynamic competitive environments, SWOT alone is inadequate — it must be supplemented by more rigorous analytical tools. The most significant limitation is its static nature: strategy must be a continuous process, not a one-off exercise. Whether SWOT is "valuable" ultimately depends on how it is used: as one input among many into an ongoing strategic process, it adds genuine value. As a standalone tool expected to produce strategic answers on its own, it falls short. The effectiveness of SWOT outweighs its limitations for businesses that treat it as a starting framework rather than a complete solution.

Analyse: Impact of Receivables/Payables Changes on Cash (2020 P1 Q19) [9 marks]

Model Answer Guidance

Effect of Rising Receivables Days (30 → 45)

Receivables days increasing from 30 to 45 means Wilson PLC's customers are now taking 50% longer to pay. This means cash is tied up in trade receivables for an additional 15 days — cash that could otherwise be used to pay suppliers, invest, or cover operating costs. For every £1m of daily revenue, this represents an additional £15m of cash locked in receivables. The business is essentially providing interest-free credit to its customers. This will directly reduce the cash balance on the balance sheet and could create liquidity problems, particularly if the business has fixed costs (rent, wages) that cannot be delayed. In the worst case, Wilson PLC could face a cash flow crisis despite being profitable — the classic scenario of "profit-rich but cash-poor."

Effect of Rising Payables Days (38 → 43)

Payables days rising from 38 to 43 means Wilson PLC is taking 5 days longer to pay its suppliers. This partially offsets the receivables problem because the business retains cash for longer before paying out. Slower payment to suppliers is beneficial for cash flow in the short term. However, the increase in payables days (5 days) is much SMALLER than the increase in receivables days (15 days). The net effect is negative: the business is receiving cash 15 days slower but only paying 5 days slower — a net cash flow deterioration of approximately 10 days. Furthermore, delaying supplier payments risks damaging relationships — suppliers may demand upfront payment, reduce credit terms, or prioritise other customers for deliveries.

Brief Evaluation

The combined effect on Wilson PLC's cash levels is negative. The receivables deterioration significantly outweighs the payables offset. The business should take action: tightening credit control (chasing debts faster), offering early payment discounts to customers, or using debt factoring to convert receivables into immediate cash. Without intervention, the cash position will continue to decline even if sales and profits remain stable.

Essay: "Are financial ratios the best way of assessing the performance of non-profit organisations?" [25 marks] — 2024 P1 Q21

Model Answer Guidance

Introduction

Financial ratios such as ROCE, current ratio, and gearing are designed to measure the financial health and efficiency of profit-making businesses. Non-profit organisations (NPOs) — charities, mutuals, social enterprises — have fundamentally different primary objectives: they exist to serve a social mission rather than maximise shareholder wealth. The question is whether tools designed for one context can be meaningfully applied to another.

Argument FOR — Financial Discipline Still Matters

Even NPOs must manage their finances effectively to survive and fulfil their mission. A charity that runs out of cash cannot help anyone. Liquidity ratios (current ratio) tell an NPO whether it can meet short-term obligations — essential for maintaining operations. Gearing indicates whether an NPO is dangerously reliant on debt. Efficiency ratios (payables/receivables days) show how well working capital is managed. ROCE can be adapted: instead of measuring profit generation, it measures how effectively capital is deployed to generate income (donations, grants, trading income) to fund charitable activities. Oxfam, for example, publishes financial ratios in its annual report to demonstrate accountability to donors. Financial ratios provide transparency, enable comparison with similar organisations, and signal to donors/funders that money is being managed responsibly.

Counter-argument — Financial Ratios Miss the Primary Purpose

However, the "best" measure must capture what the organisation is actually trying to achieve. For a charity focused on homelessness, the most meaningful measures might be: number of people housed, reduction in rough sleeping, long-term outcomes for service users. For a mutual like John Lewis, employee satisfaction, member dividends, and community contribution matter more than ROCE. Financial ratios measure financial HEALTH but not MISSION ACHIEVEMENT — and for NPOs, mission achievement IS the primary purpose. A charity could have excellent financial ratios (high liquidity, low gearing) while completely failing to deliver its social objectives — perhaps because it is hoarding donations rather than spending them on beneficiaries. Elkington's Triple Bottom Line, which measures profit, people, AND planet, provides a more holistic framework that aligns better with NPO objectives.

Counter-argument — Alternative Performance Measures

Non-financial metrics are arguably more relevant: beneficiary outcomes, volunteer engagement, donor satisfaction, impact per £ spent, social return on investment (SROI). These directly measure whether the organisation is fulfilling its reason for existing. The Charity Commission assesses charities on their public benefit delivery, not their ROCE. Balanced approaches that COMBINE financial ratios with social impact measures are likely to be more useful than either alone.

Judgement — Weighted

Financial ratios are a NECESSARY but NOT SUFFICIENT tool for assessing NPO performance. They ensure financial sustainability and accountability — without which no mission can be fulfilled. However, they are not the "best" way because they cannot capture the social, environmental, or community outcomes that define an NPO's success. The most effective approach combines financial ratios (to ensure viability) with mission-specific outcome measures (to assess impact). The relative importance of financial ratios depends on the type of NPO: a large charity receiving public funding needs robust financial reporting; a small community group may find detailed ratio analysis disproportionate to its scale. For NPOs facing financial difficulty, ratios become more important as a survival tool. In stable times, outcome measures outweigh financial metrics as the primary indicator of success. Financial ratios are essential but secondary — the "best" assessment is one that measures what the organisation was created to achieve.

2023 P1 Q6 — Government Policy: GDP + Inflation

“Which government policy is most likely to increase BOTH GDP and inflation?”

A. Higher interest rates
B. Higher tax on goods
C. Lower income taxes  ✔
D. More open trade

Correct Answer: C — Lower income taxes

Why this is correct: Lower income taxes = more disposable income → more spending → GDP rises. BUT increased demand pushes prices up → inflation rises too.

Examiner Trap
Open trade increases competition which REDUCES inflation. Only lower taxes boost BOTH GDP and inflation simultaneously.
Strategy Tip
Expansionary fiscal policy (lower taxes, higher spending) boosts GDP AND inflation. Contractionary does the opposite.

2024 P1 Q12 — Exchange Rate Rises

“The exchange rate for the £ increases. This will lead to:”

A. Import costs decrease, export demand decreases  ✔
B. Import costs decrease, export demand increases
C. Import costs increase, export demand decreases
D. Import costs increase, export demand increases

Correct Answer: A — Import costs decrease, export demand decreases

Why this is correct: Stronger £ = each £ buys more foreign currency → imports CHEAPER. BUT UK goods cost MORE in foreign currency → export demand FALLS.

Examiner Trap
A “strong” pound helps importers but HURTS exporters. Always check BOTH effects.
Strategy Tip
£ rises = imports cheaper, exports dearer. £ falls = imports dearer, exports cheaper. This rule never changes.

2025 P1 Q3 — GDP + Exchange Rate → Imports

“Which option will lead to the LARGEST increase in UK imports?”

A. Fall in GDP and fall in exchange rate
B. Fall in GDP and rise in exchange rate
C. Rise in GDP and fall in exchange rate
D. Rise in GDP and rise in exchange rate  ✔

Correct Answer: D — Rise in GDP AND rise in exchange rate

Why this is correct: Rising GDP = buy more imports. Rising £ = imports cheaper. Both forces push imports UP = LARGEST increase.

Examiner Trap
GDP growth drives import demand independently of the exchange rate — a wealthier country buys more from abroad.
Strategy Tip
For “largest” questions, find where BOTH factors push in the SAME direction.

2020 P1 Q9 — ARR Calculation

Year 0: (£1,000k). Year 1: £500k. Year 2: £900k. “After two years, the ARR is...”

A. 90%
B. 70%
C. 40%
D. 20%  ✔

Correct Answer: D — 20%

Why this is correct: Total returns = 500 + 900 = 1,400. Profit = 1,400 − 1,000 = 400. Average annual profit = 400 ÷ 2 = 200. ARR = (200 ÷ 1,000) × 100 = 20%.

Examiner Trap
90% = not subtracting investment. 70% = total not average. 40% = wrong denominator. Follow: subtract, average, THEN divide by investment.
Strategy Tip
ARR = (Average annual profit ÷ Initial investment) × 100.

2017 P1 Q7 — Sensitivity Analysis Definition

“An approach to examine the impact of possible changes on the predicted financial outcome of an investment.”

A. Investment criteria
B. Non-financial factors
C. Sensitivity analysis  ✔
D. The discount rate

Correct Answer: C — Sensitivity analysis

Why this is correct: “Impact of possible changes” = what-if scenarios. “Predicted financial outcome” = the investment result.

Examiner Trap
The discount rate is ONE input. Sensitivity analysis tests what happens when ANY input changes.
Strategy Tip
Sensitivity analysis = “what if?” testing. Changes ONE variable at a time to assess risk.

2018 P1 Q2 — SWOT Classification

“In a SWOT analysis, which statement is true?”

A. Inefficient QA system is a threat
B. Lower unemployment leading to increased demand is a strength
C. New legislation requiring product modifications is a weakness
D. The liquidation of a competitor is an opportunity  ✔

Correct Answer: D — Liquidation of competitor is an opportunity

Why this is correct: Competitor closing = external event that benefits you = opportunity. A is wrong (QA is internal = weakness). B is wrong (unemployment is external = opportunity). C is wrong (legislation is external = threat).

Examiner Trap
The #1 SWOT error: confusing internal (S/W) with external (O/T). “Can the business control this?” Yes = internal. No = external.
Strategy Tip
S/W = INSIDE (you control it). O/T = OUTSIDE (you can’t). Then: good = S or O. Bad = W or T.

2017 P1 Q4 — Gearing Calculation

Current assets: £3m. Current liabilities: £2.4m. NCL: £4.2m. CE: £12m.

A. 35%  ✔
B. 250%
C. 20%
D. 5%

Correct Answer: A — 35%

Why this is correct: Gearing = NCL ÷ CE × 100 = 4.2 ÷ 12 × 100 = 35%.

Examiner Trap
250% = dividing CE by NCL (upside down). 20% = using current liabilities. Always: NCL ÷ CE.
Strategy Tip
Below 50% = low gearing. Above 50% = high gearing = vulnerable to interest rate rises.

2025 P1 Q7 — Gross Profit Calculation NEW

Revenue: £20m. COS: £6m. Expenses: £4m. Tax: £7m.

A. £3m
B. £10m
C. £14m  ✔
D. £16m

Correct Answer: C — £14m

Why this is correct: Gross profit = Revenue − COS = 20 − 6 = £14m. Do NOT subtract expenses or tax.

Examiner Trap
£3m = profit for the year. £10m = operating profit. The question asks for GROSS profit = Revenue − COS only.
Strategy Tip
Revenue − COS = Gross. Gross − Expenses = Operating. Operating − Tax = Profit for year. Know which level is asked.
Calculations Financial Ratios & Investment Appraisal

Current Ratio — 2025 P1 Q16

Inventories £80m, Receivables £35m, Cash £40m, Payables £70m, NCL £230m, Equity £190m.

Full Working

Step 1: Current assets = Inventories + Receivables + Cash = 80 + 35 + 40 = £155m

Step 2: Current liabilities = Payables = £70m

Step 3: Current ratio = 155 ÷ 70 = 2.21:1

Note: The balance sheet shows net current assets of £85m (155 − 70), which confirms our calculation. A ratio of 2.21:1 is healthy — the business can comfortably cover short-term debts.

ROCE (Working Backwards) — 2025 P1 Q17

ROCE = 12%. Capital employed = Total equity + NCL = 190 + 230 = £420m.

Full Working

Step 1: ROCE = Operating profit ÷ Capital employed × 100

Step 2: 12 = Operating profit ÷ 420 × 100

Step 3: Operating profit = 12 × 420 ÷ 100 = £50.4m

Gearing After New Loan — 2019 P1 Q17

NCL = £200,000. Gearing = 40%. Takes out £100,000 long-term loan. Find new gearing.

Full Working

Step 1: Original CE = NCL ÷ Gearing = 200,000 ÷ 0.40 = £500,000

Step 2: New NCL = 200,000 + 100,000 = £300,000

Step 3: New CE = 500,000 + 100,000 = £600,000 (loan adds to both NCL and CE)

Step 4: New gearing = 300,000 ÷ 600,000 × 100 = 50%

NPV — 2024 P1 Q17

Investment: £20m in Year 0. Year 1: £11m (DF 0.91). Year 2: £12m (DF 0.83).

Full Working

Year 0: −20 × 1.00 = −£20m

Year 1: 11 × 0.91 = £10.01m

Year 2: 12 × 0.83 = £9.96m

NPV = −20 + 10.01 + 9.96 = −£0.03m (approximately zero / marginally negative)

A marginally negative NPV means the project barely fails to meet the required rate of return. The business should consider whether qualitative factors (strategic positioning, market entry) justify proceeding despite the borderline financial case.

Payback — 2017 P1 Q16

Year 0: −£50k. Year 1: net return £13k. Year 2: net return £28k. Year 3: net return £27k.

Full Working

Cumulative: End Y0: −50. End Y1: −50 + 13 = −37. End Y2: −37 + 28 = −9. End Y3: −9 + 27 = +18.

Payback occurs during Year 3.

Interpolation: £9k remaining at start of Y3. Y3 cash flow = £27k.

Months into Y3 = (9 ÷ 27) × 12 = 4 months.

Payback = 2 years and 4 months.

Calculation Exam Technique for Unit 3.7
1. Gearing: use NON-CURRENT liabilities only, NOT total liabilities. Capital employed = Equity + NCL.
2. ROCE: Operating profit, NOT profit for the year (don't subtract tax or interest).
3. Current ratio: Include ALL current assets (inventories + receivables + cash). Current liabilities include overdraft and payables.
4. NPV: Multiply EVERY year by its discount factor, including Year 0 (factor = 1.00). Don't forget negative cash flows.
5. ARR: Average ANNUAL profit = (Total returns − Investment) ÷ Number of years. Then ÷ Investment × 100.
6. Payables days: divide by COST OF SALES (not revenue). Receivables days: divide by REVENUE (not cost of sales).
7. Always state UNITS: ratios as X:1, percentages with %, days as "days," money with £.
