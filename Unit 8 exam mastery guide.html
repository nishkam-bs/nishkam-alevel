Examiner Trap #1 — Product Development vs Diversification: The Most Repeated AQA Trap
This trap appears in 2017, 2022, and 2023. The distinction is CRITICAL:

Product development = New product, EXISTING market and EXISTING customers.
Diversification = New product, NEW market or NEW type of customer.

2023 P1 Q3: “Women’s clothes store adds FURNITURE for EXISTING CUSTOMERS.” = Diversification — furniture is a completely different product market from clothing, even though customers overlap.
2022 P1 Q7: “Hot drinks café adds CAKES.” = Product development — cakes are closely related to the existing café market.
2017 P1 Q8: “Starbucks opens TEA SHOPS for existing customers.” = Product development — tea is related to their core beverage offering.

The rule: If the new product is CLOSELY RELATED (cakes with coffee, tea with coffee), it’s product development. If it’s a COMPLETELY DIFFERENT category (furniture in a clothes shop), it’s diversification — even if the same people buy it.
Examiner Trap #2 — Ansoff Definitions: Statement Format (2024 P1 Q6, 2019 P1 Q8)
2024: S1: “Market development = new product, new market.” = FALSE (that’s diversification). S2: “Penetration = existing product, existing market.” = TRUE. Answer: C.
2019: S1: “Penetration = existing product in new market.” = FALSE (that’s market development). S2: “Diversification = new product OR new market.” = FALSE (needs BOTH). Answer: D.
Golden rule: Diversification = new product AND new market. Market development = existing product, new market. Product development = new product, existing market. Penetration = existing product, existing market.
Examiner Trap #3 — Boston Matrix Movement (2020 P1 Q2, 2025 P1 Q4) NEW
2020: A product’s market share falls from 60%→50% despite revenue increasing. The market is growing. Market share is still highest = still market leader. High growth + high market share = star. The trap: students see “market share fell” and assume it’s a dog. But it’s STILL the market leader — share fell only because the market grew faster.
2025: Product X was market leader in a low-growth market (= cash cow). Competitors promote, market grows rapidly, but X retains leadership. Now: high growth + high share = star. Answer: B — cash cow to star. Products move between quadrants when market growth rate OR relative market share changes.
How 3.8.1 Ansoff’s Matrix Comes Up in the Exam — What to Expect & What to Do
Paper 1 MCQs (1 mark): Ansoff classification MCQs appear almost every year (2017, 2019, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025). The trap is ALWAYS product development vs diversification. Boston Matrix movement MCQs also appear (2020, 2025). Action: draw the 2×2 grid before answering. Ask: is the product new or existing? Is the market new or existing? For Boston Matrix: check BOTH growth rate AND relative market share.

Paper 1 Essays (25 marks): NPD vs market development risk (2018). MNC expansion to increase profits (2025). Action: argue both sides using the Ansoff risk framework. Conclude with “depends on the business’s existing capabilities and market conditions.”

Paper 2 Analyse (9 marks): Product portfolio actions using Boston Matrix (2017). Organisational design for product portfolio (2024). Action: link Ansoff strategy to specific case study data. Explain WHY the business chose that direction.

Paper 3 Evaluate (20 marks): Growth option choice (2024 P3 Q5). Action: compare both options using quantitative data (investment appraisal) AND qualitative factors (risk, fit with objectives, competitive environment). Reach a justified recommendation.

Examiner Trap #4 — Cost Leadership ≠ Cheapest Price (2025 P1 Q2)
Cost leadership means being the lowest-cost PRODUCER, NOT necessarily charging the lowest PRICE. The 2025 MCQ asks which helps achieve DIFFERENTIATION. Answer: B — Advertising leading to higher added value. Economies of scale (A), cheaper suppliers (C), and higher labour productivity (D) all reduce COSTS — they support cost leadership, not differentiation. Differentiation is about increasing PERCEIVED VALUE, not reducing costs.
Examiner Trap #5 — Quality and Competitive Advantage (2022 P1 Q10, 2024 P1 Q11) NEW
2024: S1: “Quality → price inelastic” = TRUE. Better quality creates loyalty and fewer substitutes, meaning customers are less sensitive to price changes. S2: “Quality → competitive advantage” = TRUE. Superior quality differentiates the product. Answer: A — Both true.
2022: S1: “Quality → price elastic” = FALSE. Quality makes demand MORE inelastic, not elastic. Better quality = less price sensitivity. S2: “Quality → added value” = TRUE. Higher quality increases the gap between perceived value and cost. Answer: C — S1 false, S2 true.
The rule: Quality → inelastic (not elastic). Quality → added value (always). Quality → competitive advantage (always). These three links are tested repeatedly.
Examiner Trap #6 — “Stuck in the Middle” — Porter’s Danger Zone
Porter warned that businesses trying BOTH cost leadership AND differentiation without committing end up “stuck in the middle” — no clear advantage. They are outperformed by focused competitors on both dimensions. However, some modern businesses combine elements successfully (IKEA: low cost + Scandinavian design; Zara: low cost + fast fashion). In essays, acknowledge “stuck in the middle” as a risk but evaluate whether some businesses have overcome it.
How 3.8.2 Porter’s / Competitive Advantage Comes Up in the Exam — What to Expect & What to Do
Paper 1 MCQs (1 mark): Porter’s differentiation vs cost leadership (2025 Q2). Quality → price inelasticity + competitive advantage (2022, 2024). Niche market advantages (2022). Barriers to entry (2019). Low supplier power → cost leadership (2025). Action: know the distinction between cost-reducing actions (support cost leadership) and value-adding actions (support differentiation).

Paper 1 Essays (25 marks): Low-cost vs differentiation sustainability (2019). Low cost to differentiation transition (2020). Finance function → lowest cost (2022). Continued investment difficulty (2025). Quality → profits (2023). QA vs QC (2025). Action: these are the highest-frequency essay topics in the entire spec. Use two arguments with evaluations. Conclude with a weighted judgement that distinguishes by industry type, business size, and competitive context.

Paper 1 Analyse (9 marks): Large business → niche market (2023). Action: explain higher margins in niche + diversification benefits. Evaluate with niche risks.

Paper 2 Evaluate (16–20 marks): Big four vs discounters (2017). Price cutting for cap util (2017). NCA utilisation for low-cost advantage (2025). Action: for “all businesses” questions, contrast by business type (cost leader vs differentiator), size, and market conditions.

Paper 3 Essay (24 marks): Innovation essential for differentiation? (2024). Action: argue innovation is essential for TECH-driven differentiation but NOT for heritage/luxury/service-based differentiation. Conclude: “depends on the source of differentiation.”

Examiner Trap #7 — Niche Market Advantages MCQ (2022 P1 Q13)
“Which is LEAST likely to be an advantage of operating in a niche market for a small business?” Answer: A — Economies of scope. Economies of scope come from producing a RANGE of products — the opposite of niche specialisation. A niche business is focused and narrow; it doesn’t have the product range for scope economies.

Examiner Trap #8 — “QA ALWAYS Leads to Greater Profits” (2025 P1 Q24)
The word “always” is the signal. QA CAN lead to greater profits through lower waste, fewer returns, stronger reputation. BUT: (1) QA requires significant upfront investment — small businesses may not afford it. (2) QA takes TIME before benefits materialise. (3) In price-sensitive markets, QA costs may not be recovered. (4) QC may be more cost-effective for simple products. Judgement: QA is more likely to lead to greater profits for businesses pursuing differentiation, but is not always superior — it depends on market context, size, and resources.

Competitive Advantage — Benefits & Difficulties of Maintaining

Benefits of Competitive Advantage
Higher profit margins — premium pricing (differentiation) or lower costs (cost leadership).
Customer loyalty — unique offering creates switching costs.
Barriers to entry — strong brand, patents, economies of scale deter new competitors.
Market share growth — superior offering attracts customers from rivals.
Pricing power — ability to raise prices without losing significant volume.
Difficulties of Maintaining Competitive Advantage
Imitation — Competitors copy innovations, designs, processes. First-mover advantage erodes.
Technological change — New tech can make existing advantages obsolete (Nokia → smartphones).
Changing consumer tastes — What differentiates today may not matter tomorrow.
Complacency — Successful businesses may stop innovating.
Cost of investment — Maintaining advantage requires CONTINUOUS investment in R&D, marketing, technology.
Globalisation — International competitors may have lower cost bases or superior technology.
Examiner Trap #9 — Barriers to Entry MCQ (2019 P1 Q15)
“Which is NOT a barrier to entry?” Answer: B — Market leader experiences diseconomies of scale. Diseconomies make the incumbent LESS efficient — this HELPS new entrants because the market leader is becoming weaker. Vertical integration (A), brand loyalty (C), and patents (D) all make entry harder.

Essay: “Which competitive advantage — low-cost or differentiation — is easier for a UK business to maintain?” [25] — 2019 P1 Q22

Model Answer Guidance

Introduction — Standard Version

(Porter’s Generic Strategies) argues that competitive advantage stems from either being the lowest-cost producer (cost leadership) or offering something uniquely valuable (differentiation). “Easier to maintain” requires assessment of how sustainable each type is against competitive threats, changing conditions, and the resources required over time. For UK businesses, high labour costs, a strong but volatile currency, and sophisticated consumers all shape the answer.

Introduction — PBA Challenge Version

P: One could argue that differentiation is easier to maintain because it builds intangible assets — brand loyalty, reputation, organisational culture — that competitors cannot quickly replicate. B: However, cost leadership can also be durable when built on structural advantages like economies of scale, proprietary technology, or supply chain infrastructure that take years to develop. A: Ultimately, this essay will argue that for most UK businesses, differentiation is more sustainable, but it depends on the specific industry, business size, and source of the advantage.

Argument FOR Differentiation Being Easier to Maintain — Version A: Explicit Chains

One could argue that differentiation creates more durable competitive advantage because it is built on intangible assets that are inherently difficult to copy, this means that while a competitor can match a cost leader’s price within months, replicating decades of brand equity takes far longer. Therefore, businesses like Dyson that differentiate through continuous product innovation protected by 12,000+ patents create legal barriers that prevent direct imitation (Porter’s Five Forces — barriers to entry), as a result maintaining premium pricing and higher profit margins even as competitors enter the market. Furthermore, UK businesses have natural strengths in differentiation — a reputation for quality (Rolls-Royce, Burberry), creative industries leadership, and service excellence — causing differentiation to align more naturally with the UK’s competitive position than cost leadership in a high-wage economy. This thus leads to the conclusion that differentiation is easier for a UK business to maintain because it creates barriers to imitation that cost advantages cannot match.

Evaluation

However, this may not be the case if consumer tastes change rapidly or if the business fails to invest continuously in the source of its differentiation. This is likely to depend on whether the differentiation is based on innovation (which requires ongoing R&D spend) or heritage (which is more naturally self-sustaining).

Argument FOR Cost Leadership Being Easier to Maintain — Version A: Explicit Chains

On the other hand, one could contend that cost leadership built on structural advantages can be equally durable, this means that businesses like Primark that have built unique supply chain infrastructure over decades create advantages that cannot be quickly replicated. Therefore, the combination of minimal advertising, basic store design, and enormous purchasing power creates a cost structure that competitors would need years and billions of pounds to match (Economies of Scale). Furthermore, cost leadership is less vulnerable to changing consumer tastes — customers always value lower prices, regardless of trends — causing cost leaders to maintain relevance even during economic downturns when consumers trade down from premium brands. This thus leads to the recognition that cost leadership may be easier to maintain when it is built on structural infrastructure rather than just operational efficiency.

Evaluation

However, this assumes that the cost advantage is structural rather than temporary. Many cost-saving techniques (lean production, automation) can be copied by competitors within years. This could be less significant if the business operates in an industry where scale itself is the primary barrier to entry.

Conclusion

Overall, one could argue that for UK businesses specifically, differentiation is more likely to be easier to maintain than cost leadership.

While both types can be sustained, the most significant factor that needs to be considered is the UK’s high cost base, which creates a structural disadvantage for cost leadership strategies while the UK’s strengths in innovation, brand-building, and service excellence provide natural advantages for differentiation.

But ultimately, the ability to create capabilities that are genuinely difficult to replicate carries greater weight than the type of advantage itself. Where imitation is easy, advantage is temporary regardless of type. Where imitation is hard, advantage is durable.

Therefore, differentiation is easier for most UK businesses to maintain because intangible assets like brand loyalty and organisational culture are harder to copy than cost-reduction techniques — but this is not universal, and large-scale cost leaders like Primark demonstrate that structural cost advantages can also endure.

Analyse: Why a Large Business Might Introduce a Product into a Niche Market [9] — 2023 P1 Q18

Model Answer Guidance

Paragraph 1 — Higher Profit Margins Through Targeted Premium Pricing

One could argue that a niche market allows a large business to charge premium prices because the product is tailored to specific customer needs that mass-market products don’t serve, this means that customers in niche markets are typically less price-sensitive — they value specialisation, exclusivity, or precise fit-for-purpose more than price (PED — price inelastic). Therefore, for a large business, the infrastructure (distribution, brand recognition, supply chain) already exists, so the incremental cost of entering a niche is relatively low, as a result the margin per unit can be significantly higher than in its mass market. This can be explored through Volkswagen Group, which owns Bugatti and Lamborghini (ultra-luxury niche) alongside mass-market VW and Skoda — the niche brands generate much higher margins per unit, boosting overall group profitability despite low volumes.

Paragraph 2 — Diversification of Revenue and Risk Reduction

One might also suggest that entering a niche market allows a large business to diversify its revenue streams (Ansoff’s Matrix — diversification), this means that reducing dependence on its core mass market, which may face a downturn, recession, or increased competition. Furthermore, niche markets often respond differently to economic cycles — luxury niches may be recession-resistant because wealthy customers maintain spending, while budget niches may grow during downturns as consumers trade down. This thus leads to the recognition that a large business can also use niche products to test innovations before rolling them to the mass market, reducing risk by trialling new concepts with a smaller, more engaged audience first.

MCQ MCQ Masterclass — Every 3.8 MCQ Decoded

2017 P1 Q8 — Starbucks Tea Shops

“Starbucks opens 300 tea shops in America to appeal to existing customers.”

A. Diversification
B. Market development
C. Market penetration
D. Product development  ✔

Correct Answer: D — Product development

Why this is correct: Tea is a new PRODUCT. America is an EXISTING market. Existing customers targeted. New product + existing market = product development. Tea and coffee are within the same café/beverage market.

Examiner Trap
Students who think “tea shops” = a completely new business = diversification are wrong. Tea is closely related to Starbucks’ core offering.
Strategy Tip
Ask: is the new product in a RELATED or UNRELATED category? Related = product development. Unrelated = diversification.

2019 P1 Q8 — Ansoff Definitions (Both False)

S1: “Market penetration = existing product in a new market.”
S2: “Diversification = developing a new product OR entering a new market.”

A. Both true
B. S1 true, S2 false
C. S1 false, S2 true
D. Both false  ✔

Correct Answer: D — Both false

Why this is correct: S1 describes market DEVELOPMENT, not penetration. S2 is wrong because diversification requires BOTH new product AND new market — not “or.”

Examiner Trap
The word “OR” in S2 is the trap. Diversification = AND (both new). If only one is new, it’s product development or market development.
Strategy Tip
Memorise: Penetration = both existing. Product dev = new product only. Market dev = new market only. Diversification = BOTH new.

2022 P1 Q7 — Café Adds Cakes

“Hot drinks café adds cakes based on customer focus group feedback.”

A. Diversification, quantitative
B. Diversification, qualitative
C. Product development, qualitative  ✔
D. Product development, quantitative

Correct Answer: C — Product development from qualitative research

Why this is correct: Cakes = closely related product in same market (café sector). New product + existing market = product development. Focus groups = opinions/views = qualitative.

Examiner Trap
Double-check BOTH halves: (1) Ansoff category (2) Research type. Students who get the Ansoff right may pick quantitative by mistake.
Strategy Tip
Focus groups = qualitative (opinions). Surveys with numbers = quantitative. Always identify which before answering.

2023 P1 Q3 — Clothing Store Adds Furniture

“A women’s clothes store adds furniture to increase spending by existing customers.”

A. Diversification  ✔
B. Market development
C. Market penetration
D. Product development

Correct Answer: A — Diversification

Why this is correct: Furniture is a completely different product market from clothing. Even though existing customers buy it, the PRODUCT MARKET (home furnishing) is entirely new for this business = diversification.

Examiner Trap
Students argue “it’s for existing customers so it’s product development.” But Ansoff defines strategy by PRODUCT MARKET, not just by who buys it.
Strategy Tip
The test is: are you entering a new PRODUCT MARKET? Clothing → furniture = yes = diversification. Coffee → cakes = no = product development.

2024 P1 Q6 — Ansoff Definitions

S1: “Market development = selling a new product in a new market.”
S2: “Market penetration = selling an existing product in an existing market.”

A. Both true
B. S1 true, S2 false
C. S1 false, S2 true  ✔
D. Both false

Correct Answer: C — S1 false, S2 true

Why this is correct: S1 describes DIVERSIFICATION, not market development. Market development = EXISTING product in a NEW market. S2 is the textbook definition of penetration.

Examiner Trap
“Market DEVELOPMENT” does NOT mean “developing a new product.” It means developing new MARKETS for existing products.
Strategy Tip
The name tells you what’s NEW. Market development = developing the MARKET (new market). Product development = developing the PRODUCT (new product).

2025 P1 Q5 — Ansoff Risk Ranking

S1: “Diversification is the most risky.” S2: “Market penetration is the least risky.”

A. Both true  ✔
B. S1 true, S2 false
C. S1 false, S2 true
D. Both false

Correct Answer: A — Both true

Why this is correct: Diversification = highest risk (both product and market are unfamiliar). Penetration = lowest risk (both are known). This is the simplest possible Ansoff MCQ.

Examiner Trap
No real trap here — just know the risk hierarchy cold.
Strategy Tip
Risk ranking: Penetration (lowest) → Product dev / Market dev (medium) → Diversification (highest). Free mark.

2025 P1 Q4 — Boston Matrix: Cash Cow to Star NEW

“Product X was market leader in a low growth market. Competitors promote, market grows rapidly, X retains leadership.”

A. Cash cow to question mark
B. Cash cow to star  ✔
C. Question mark to star
D. Star to cash cow

Correct Answer: B — Cash cow to star

Why this is correct: Originally: high share + low growth = cash cow. After: market grows rapidly + X retains leadership = high share + high growth = star.

Examiner Trap
Students may think competitors promoting means X loses share. But the question says X “retains its market leadership” — share stays high.
Strategy Tip
Boston Matrix positions change when either market GROWTH or relative MARKET SHARE changes. Check which variable moved.

2025 P1 Q2 — Porter’s Differentiation

“Which would help achieve a differentiation strategy?”

A. Achieving economies of scale
B. Advertising leading to higher added value  ✔
C. Finding cheaper suppliers
D. Higher labour productivity

Correct Answer: B — Advertising leading to higher added value

Why this is correct: Differentiation = creating unique perceived value. Advertising builds brand and perceived quality = higher added value. A, C, D all reduce COSTS = cost leadership, not differentiation.

Examiner Trap
Students may pick A (economies of scale) thinking “bigger = better.” But EoS reduce costs — they support cost leadership, not differentiation.
Strategy Tip
Cost-reducing actions = cost leadership. Value-adding actions = differentiation. Advertising ADDS value; EoS REDUCE cost.

2025 P1 Q14 — Supplier Power & Barriers to Entry NEW

S1: “Low supplier power may encourage a retailer to use a cost leadership strategy.”
S2: “A market leader may use a low-price strategy to create a barrier to entry.”

A. Both true  ✔
B. S1 true, S2 false
C. S1 false, S2 true
D. Both false

Correct Answer: A — Both true

Why this is correct: S1 TRUE: low supplier power = retailers can negotiate lower input costs = supports cost leadership strategy. S2 TRUE: a market leader can set prices so low that new entrants cannot compete profitably = limit pricing = barrier to entry.

Examiner Trap
Students may think low prices can’t be a “barrier.” But limit pricing (setting prices below what new entrants can match) is a classic barrier strategy.
Strategy Tip
Low supplier power → lower input costs → enables cost leadership. Low pricing by incumbent → deters entry → barrier. Both are standard Porter logic.

2022 P1 Q13 — Niche Market Advantages

“Which is LEAST likely to be an advantage of a niche market for a small business?”

A. Economies of scope  ✔
B. Focused marketing
C. Less competition
D. Opportunity for tailor-made products

Correct Answer: A — Economies of scope

Why this is correct: Economies of scope = producing a WIDE RANGE of products. Niche businesses are SPECIALISED and NARROW — the opposite of scope. B, C, D are all genuine niche advantages.

Examiner Trap
Don’t confuse economies of SCOPE (range of products) with economies of SCALE (volume of one product). Neither applies strongly to a small niche business, but scope is the LEAST relevant.
Strategy Tip
Niche = narrow focus, few products, specialised. Scope = wide range, many products, shared resources. They are opposites.

2019 P1 Q15 — Barriers to Entry

“Which is NOT a barrier to entry?”

A. Vertically integrated with suppliers
B. Experiences diseconomies of scale  ✔
C. High brand loyalty
D. Owns a patent

Correct Answer: B — Diseconomies of scale

Why this is correct: Diseconomies = rising unit costs = incumbent is getting LESS efficient. This HELPS new entrants, not hinders them. A, C, D all make entry harder.

Examiner Trap
Students assume anything about the market leader = a barrier. But diseconomies are a WEAKNESS that new entrants can exploit.
Strategy Tip
Barriers PROTECT the incumbent. Diseconomies WEAKEN the incumbent. They are opposites. If the question asks what is NOT a barrier, look for a weakness.

2024 P1 Q11 — Quality → Price Inelastic + Competitive Advantage NEW

S1: “A business might improve quality to make demand more price inelastic.”
S2: “A business might improve quality to achieve a competitive advantage.”

A. Both true  ✔
B. S1 true, S2 false
C. S1 false, S2 true
D. Both false

Correct Answer: A — Both true

Why this is correct: S1 TRUE: higher quality creates loyalty and fewer substitutes = less price sensitivity = more inelastic. S2 TRUE: superior quality differentiates the product from competitors = competitive advantage.

Examiner Trap
Compare with 2022 Q10 which says quality → price ELASTIC (false). Quality makes demand MORE inelastic, never more elastic.
Strategy Tip
Quality → inelastic (always). Quality → added value (always). Quality → competitive advantage (always). Learn these three links.

2022 P1 Q10 — Quality → Added Value NEW

S1: “A business might improve quality to make demand more price elastic.”
S2: “A business might improve quality to increase added value.”

A. Both true
B. S1 true, S2 false
C. S1 false, S2 true  ✔
D. Both false

Correct Answer: C — S1 false, S2 true

Why this is correct: S1 FALSE: quality makes demand MORE INELASTIC (less sensitive to price), not more elastic. S2 TRUE: higher quality increases perceived value relative to cost = higher added value.

Examiner Trap
The trap is the word “elastic” in S1. Students who don’t think carefully may assume quality + price sensitivity are linked positively. They are linked NEGATIVELY.
Strategy Tip
Higher quality = customers value the product MORE = they are LESS willing to switch = LESS price sensitive = MORE inelastic. This logic never changes.
Summary Unit 3.8 — Final Exam Reminders
The 16 Non-Negotiable Rules for Unit 3.8
1. Market penetration = existing product + existing market = LOWEST RISK. Diversification = new product + new market = HIGHEST RISK.
2. Diversification requires BOTH new product AND new market. Just one = product or market development.
3. If the new product is a COMPLETELY DIFFERENT category (furniture in a clothes shop) = diversification. If CLOSELY RELATED (cakes with coffee) = product development.
4. Boston Matrix positions change when market growth rate or relative market share changes. Cash cow → star if market growth increases. Tested 2020, 2025.
5. Cost leadership = lowest cost PRODUCER, not necessarily lowest PRICE. A cost leader can charge market price for higher margins.
6. Differentiation = creating uniqueness through brand, quality, innovation, or service that customers pay a PREMIUM for.
7. “Stuck in the middle” = trying both cost leadership AND differentiation without committing = no clear advantage.
8. Niche = small, specialised market with higher margins and less competition. Economies of SCOPE are NOT a niche advantage (2022 MCQ).
9. Barriers to entry PROTECT incumbents. Diseconomies of scale HELP entrants — they are NOT a barrier (2019 MCQ).
10. Quality → price INELASTIC (not elastic). Quality → added value. Quality → competitive advantage. Tested 2022 AND 2024.
11. Quality assurance builds quality in at every stage. Quality control inspects at the end. QA is better for differentiation but costs more upfront.
12. Maintaining competitive advantage requires CONTINUOUS investment — but direction of investment matters more than amount.
13. Innovation can be product (new products) or process (new methods). Both create competitive advantage.
14. Low supplier power enables cost leadership (cheaper inputs). Low pricing by incumbent creates barrier to entry. Both tested 2025.
15. In “all businesses” questions: ALWAYS contrast by size, market, industry, and strategy.
16. Ansoff definitions MCQ format: check each statement independently. The most common errors involve confusing market development with diversification and using “OR” instead of “AND” for diversification.
