Pattern Alert — Unit 3.6
1. Taylor vs Herzberg is AQA’s MOST tested MCQ concept in 3.6. Tested 2017 and 2019. Students MUST know: piece rate = Taylor YES, Herzberg NO (hygiene factor only).
2. Employee costs as % of turnover appears in EVERY data set question. The denominator is REVENUE (not labour turnover). This is tested 2019, 2020, 2022, 2023.

3.6.1–2 HR Objectives, Hard vs Soft HRM & HR Data

HR Objectives

What HR Objectives Cover
Employee engagement and involvement — ensuring workers feel valued and connected to business goals.
Talent development — training, upskilling and retaining key employees.
Diversity — building a workforce representative of the society and markets the business serves.
Alignment with corporate objectives — HR strategy must support the wider business strategy, e.g., a business pursuing growth needs a recruitment-focused HR strategy; a business cutting costs may focus on productivity and leaner staffing.

Hard vs Soft HRM

Hard HRMSoft HRM
View of workersA cost to be minimised and controlledAn asset to be developed and invested in
Motivation approachTaylor: financial incentives, close supervision, targetsHerzberg/Maslow: enrichment, empowerment, development
Leadership styleAutocratic, directive, top-downDemocratic, participative, consultative
Employment contractsZero-hours, short-term, flexible (reduce fixed costs)Permanent, full-time, career paths
Real-world examplesAmazon warehouse (targets, monitoring, high turnover)John Lewis Partnership (profit sharing, democratic structures)
Best suited toCost-leadership strategies, price-sensitive marketsDifferentiation strategies, knowledge/creative industries
Examiner Trap #1 — Hard/Soft HRM and Profit (2017 P3 KMH plc)
“Should KMH plc switch from soft to hard HRM to boost profit?” Students who only argue FOR (lower costs, tighter control) cap at mid-band. The top-band answer evaluates: hard HRM reduces wage costs but may increase labour turnover, recruitment costs, and absenteeism — net effect on profit is NOT guaranteed to be positive. The effectiveness depends on the industry, the workforce type, and whether cost reduction or productivity is the binding constraint. Always evaluate net impact, not just the direction of change.

HR Data — Key Metrics

MetricFormulaWhat It Tells You
Labour productivityOutput ÷ Number of employeesEfficiency of the workforce. Higher = more output per worker.
Labour turnover(Number leaving ÷ Average number employed) × 100Rate at which staff leave. Higher = more instability, recruitment costs.
Labour retention rate100% − Labour turnover rate% of staff who STAY. Higher = more stability.
Employee costs as % of turnover(Total employee costs ÷ Revenue) × 100Proportion of revenue consumed by staff costs. TURNOVER here = REVENUE.
Labour cost per unitTotal labour costs ÷ Total outputUnit efficiency. Useful for comparing sites or divisions.
Examiner Trap #2 — “Turnover” Has Two Meanings
In HR data questions, “employee costs as a percentage of turnover” uses “turnover” to mean REVENUE — not labour turnover (the rate at which staff leave). This is the single most common source of error in 3.6 data questions. If you use the wrong denominator, you will get the wrong answer even if your formula and arithmetic are correct. Turnover = revenue. Always.
When Employee Costs % of Turnover Rises — 2022 P1 Q6
The ratio rises if: (1) employee costs increase (wage rise, more staff) while revenue stays constant; (2) revenue falls (lower sales, price cuts) while employee costs stay constant; or (3) both simultaneously. The most counter-intuitive scenario: a business could cut staff but if revenue falls faster, the ratio still rises. Tested 2022 P1 Q6 — the question asked which change “most likely increases” the ratio. The correct answer involved a scenario where revenue fell disproportionately.

Examiner Trap #3 — Wider Span + Productivity (2022 P1 Q20)
“Analyse how widening a manager’s span of control might affect labour productivity.” Students who write ONLY positives (more delegation → motivation → productivity) cap at mid-band. The top-band answer also covers the RISK: too-wide span means less supervision → workers may lack direction → mistakes increase → productivity could FALL. Additionally, the manager becomes overloaded → poorer quality decisions → negative impact on team performance. Always give both directions.

Real-World Examples
Redeployment — Soundcheck plc (2024 P2 Q2.2): When closing retail stores, Soundcheck redeployed staff to warehouses and online operations rather than making them redundant. Benefits: retained experienced employees who understood the brand, avoided redundancy costs, maintained morale. But: required retraining for different roles, and not all employees would suit warehouse work.

Redundancy — Carillion (2018): When Carillion collapsed, 43,000 employees faced redundancy worldwide — illustrating the human cost of business failure and why employees are stakeholders with legitimate interests in corporate decision-making.

3.6.4 Motivation: Taylor, Maslow & Herzberg + Methods

The Three Motivation Theories AQA Tests

Taylor — Scientific Management

Taylor’s Theory
Core belief: Workers are primarily motivated by MONEY. They are inherently lazy and need close supervision.
Approach: Find the most efficient way to do each task (time and motion studies) → divide work into simple, repetitive tasks → pay workers per unit produced (piece rate). Money is the primary motivator.
Payment methods Taylor would support: Piece rate, commission, performance-related pay — all link pay directly to output.
What Taylor would NOT see as motivating: Fixed salary (no link to output), job enrichment, teamwork — these don’t increase pay per unit.
Criticism: Ignores non-financial motivation; dehumanising; creates boring, repetitive work; workers may resist being treated as machines.

Maslow — Hierarchy of Needs

Diagram: Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs
Maslow’s Key Points for AQA
Needs are sequential — a hungry worker (physiological unmet) won’t be motivated by promotion (esteem). Lower needs must be satisfied first.
Self-actualisation = Reaching one’s full potential — creativity, personal growth, challenge. Never fully “met” — always drives further growth.
Business implication: Different employees are at different levels. A new graduate may prioritise safety (job security). An experienced professional may seek esteem (recognition) or self-actualisation (challenging projects). One-size-fits-all motivation fails.
Criticism: Not everyone follows the same hierarchy; cultural differences; hard to measure where someone is on the hierarchy.

Herzberg — Two-Factor Theory

Diagram: Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory
Examiner Trap #4 — The Taylor vs Herzberg Piece Rate Question (2017 P1 Q11)
Taylor: Piece rate INCREASES motivation because it links pay directly to output — exactly what Taylor advocates.
Herzberg: Piece rate does NOT increase motivation because pay is a HYGIENE FACTOR. It can prevent dissatisfaction but cannot create genuine motivation. Only motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility) create motivation.
Statement 1: “Taylor would see piece rate as increasing motivation” = TRUE.
Statement 2: “Herzberg would see piece rate as increasing motivation” = FALSE.
Answer: B. This is AQA’s most-recycled motivation MCQ concept.
Examiner Trap #5 — Maslow vs Herzberg MCQ (2020 P1 Q13)
“Maslow believed needs must be met sequentially” = TRUE. Lower needs first, then higher.
“Herzberg believed hygiene factors motivate employees” = FALSE. Hygiene factors PREVENT dissatisfaction. They do NOT motivate. Only motivators motivate.
This is the critical distinction: hygiene factors are NECESSARY but NOT SUFFICIENT. A well-paid worker in a nice office (hygiene met) is not dissatisfied — but is NOT yet motivated. Motivation requires achievement, responsibility, recognition.

Examiner Trap #6 — Empowerment MCQ (2024 P1 Q3)
“Empowerment increases motivation” = TRUE. Empowerment gives responsibility and decision-making authority — (Herzberg)’s motivators.
“Empowerment reduces the manager’s span of control” = FALSE. Empowerment does NOT change how many people report to a manager (span). It changes what authority those people have. Students confuse empowerment with delegation and both with span of control.

Conclude with a weighted judgement depending on the type of work.

For the business: Lower labour turnover (Labour Turnover) (retention savings), higher productivity (motivated engaged workers), less absenteeism, fewer disputes and strikes, smoother change management, better reputation attracting talent.

Examiner Trap #7 — “Trade Union Consultation Works for ALL Businesses” (2024 P2 Q2.3)
This 16-mark question uses “all businesses” — the guaranteed signal to discuss different contexts. Trade union consultation helps overcome resistance to change when: the workforce IS unionised, the change requires employee cooperation, and management is willing to genuinely negotiate. It does NOT work for: non-unionised workforces, businesses where rapid change is needed (consultation is slow), or owner-managed small businesses. Always contrast unionised vs non-unionised, large vs small, and different change types.
Real-World Examples
Soundcheck plc (2024 P2): Negotiated a pay agreement with trade unions during restructuring — demonstrates how consultation can build support for difficult changes. But the agreement took months to reach, during which operational efficiency suffered.

Amazon: Famously anti-union. Uses hard HRM (Hard HRM) (monitoring, targets, performance management) rather than consultation. This enables rapid decision-making but creates high labour turnover, negative press, and repeated industrial disputes — illustrating the cost of poor employer-employee relations.

John Lewis Partnership: All employees are “partners” who share profits and have a voice through democratic structures. This model creates exceptionally high engagement and loyalty — consistent with soft HRM (Soft HRM) and Herzberg’s motivators (Herzberg) — but is slower to make decisions and harder to cut costs during downturns.
How 3.6.5 Comes Up in the Exam — What to Expect & What to Do
Paper 2 Evaluate (16 marks): Trade union consultation for all businesses (2024). Action: argue FOR (collective bargaining reduces conflict, builds legitimacy for change), AGAINST (non-unionised businesses, urgent change scenarios), conclude with a weighted “depends on” judgement. Use “all businesses” logic.

Paper 3 Evaluate (16 marks): Hard vs soft HRM and profit (2017). Action: hard HRM reduces costs but may increase turnover and reduce morale. Soft HRM increases engagement but raises the wage bill. The net impact depends on the industry and workforce type.

Paper 3 Analyse (12 marks): Works councils and HR performance (2019). Action: link works councils to improved communication, reduced conflict, better change management — 7–8 chains of analysis per paragraph. Contrast with trade unions.

Essay 1: “To what extent is improved pay the best way for a PLC to improve labour productivity?” [25] — 2025 P1 Q21 NEW

Model Answer Guidance

Introduction — Standard Version

Labour productivity measures output per employee. The claim that improved pay is the “best” method to increase it assumes pay is the primary driver of worker effort and effectiveness — an assumption supported by (Taylor) but challenged by (Herzberg) and (Maslow). For a PLC with potentially thousands of employees across different roles, the answer is unlikely to be a single method.

Introduction — PBA Challenge Version

P: One could argue that improved pay is the best way for a PLC to improve labour productivity, because financial incentives directly link effort to reward — as (Taylor’s Scientific Management) argues, workers are primarily motivated by money, and piece rate or performance-related pay creates a measurable incentive to increase output. B: However, (Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory) challenges this assumption: pay is a hygiene factor that prevents dissatisfaction but does not create genuine motivation, meaning that pay rises may deliver only short-term productivity gains before becoming the new expectation. A: This essay will argue that improved pay is a necessary but insufficient condition for productivity improvement, and that non-financial methods and investment in training or technology are likely to be more effective for a large PLC in the long run.

Argument One (FOR Improved Pay) — Version A: Explicit Chains of Analysis

One could argue that improved pay is the most effective way to boost labour productivity for a PLC, this means that performance-related pay, piece rates and productivity bonuses create a direct financial incentive to increase output, linking individual effort to individual reward in a way that (Taylor’s Scientific Management) identifies as the primary driver of worker behaviour. Therefore, workers who earn more for producing more have a clear reason to raise their output levels, as a result total output rises without necessarily increasing the number of employees, improving the output-per-worker ratio that defines labour productivity. This could lead to significant competitive advantage for the PLC, as demonstrated by Amazon’s warehouse operations, where productivity bonuses and output monitoring systems drive exceptionally high units-per-hour metrics relative to industry averages — consistent with a cost leadership strategy (Porter’s Strategies) that depends on maximising throughput per employee. Furthermore, higher pay reduces labour turnover, meaning that less time and money is lost on recruitment and induction training, causing the workforce to be more experienced and more efficient on average, which further drives up output per worker across the PLC as a whole. This could lead to a reinforcing cycle where a more productive workforce generates higher revenue, enabling the PLC to sustain above-market pay levels, attracting higher-quality recruits and further improving productivity — potentially transforming pay into a Cash Cow position of competitive advantage (Boston Matrix). This thus leads to the conclusion that for repetitive, measurable roles in manufacturing or logistics, improved pay is not merely effective but potentially the most direct and controllable lever available to a PLC seeking productivity improvements, suggesting that improved pay may well be the best method in such operational contexts.

Evaluation of Argument One

However, this may not be the case if the workforce is predominantly knowledge-based, creative or managerial, where output is difficult to measure and piece rate is impractical. This is likely to depend on the nature of the work — (Taylor) is most applicable to standardised, repetitive tasks and least applicable to roles requiring creativity, judgement or collaboration. However, this assumes that higher pay permanently raises productivity, when in reality the motivational effect of a pay rise is typically temporary: within months it becomes the new expectation, and productivity returns to its previous level, meaning the PLC must repeatedly increase pay to maintain the effect — a permanently rising cost with diminishing returns.

Argument One (FOR Improved Pay) — Version B: Implicit Chains of Analysis

One might suggest that improved pay holds the greatest potential for productivity improvement in a PLC because it operates through the most fundamental driver of economic behaviour: the rational calculation of effort and reward. (Taylor’s Scientific Management) grounded this insight in empirical observation of factory workers in the early twentieth century, and the principle remains valid wherever output is measurable and individual contribution can be tied to compensation. For a large PLC with significant manufacturing or logistics operations, performance-related pay and productivity bonuses create a self-regulating system in which workers independently choose to raise their output in pursuit of higher earnings, reducing the management burden of supervision and control. Amazon’s warehouse operations exemplify this at scale — output-linked pay structures combined with real-time performance monitoring drive productivity levels that outperform competitors, underpinning the cost leadership strategy (Porter’s Strategies) that defines Amazon’s competitive positioning. Beyond direct incentives, higher pay reduces labour turnover and the associated costs of recruitment, onboarding and the productivity gap of new employees — meaning that the investment in pay delivers multiple productivity benefits simultaneously. Where current pay is below market rates and disengagement is high, a pay improvement addresses (Herzberg’s) hygiene deficiency and removes the dissatisfaction barrier that prevents other productivity initiatives from working, making it a prerequisite rather than merely an option.

Argument Two (AGAINST — Non-Financial Methods May Be More Effective) — Version A: Explicit Chains of Analysis

On the other hand, one could contend that non-financial methods of motivation are more effective than pay for improving labour productivity in a PLC, this means that job enrichment, empowerment and team working generate genuine, sustained engagement — addressing (Herzberg’s) motivators (responsibility, achievement, meaningful work) rather than merely preventing dissatisfaction. Therefore, workers in enriched roles produce higher quality output with greater personal investment, as a result labour productivity improves not just in volume but in quality and innovation — dimensions that pay-for-output fails to capture. This could lead to sustained competitive advantage for the PLC at lower ongoing cost than permanently elevated pay, as demonstrated by Google’s approach to productivity: the company invests heavily in workplace autonomy, creative freedom, psychological safety and team effectiveness rather than competing primarily on base salary, yet achieves among the highest revenue-per-employee ratios in the global technology industry — a classic Stars position on the (Boston Matrix) driven by motivated, high-performing teams. Furthermore, for a PLC employing thousands of workers across diverse roles, non-financial methods can be implemented differently for different employee groups — engineers receive autonomy, customer service teams receive recognition programmes, managers receive career development — addressing (Maslow’s) insight that workers are at different levels of need and require different motivational inputs. This could lead to a more personalised and effective motivational environment, increasing productivity across the entire workforce rather than through a uniform pay increase that may be motivating for some but irrelevant to others. This thus leads to the conclusion that non-financial methods create a virtuous cycle: engaged workers are more productive, more innovative and less likely to leave, reducing turnover costs and building organisational capability that cannot be replicated by competitors who compete purely on pay, suggesting that non-financial methods outweigh improved pay as a productivity driver for knowledge-intensive or service-oriented PLC operations.

Evaluation of Argument Two

However, the impact may be limited because non-financial methods are ineffective if pay is below the level needed to meet (Maslow’s) basic physiological and security needs — no amount of job enrichment motivates a worker who cannot afford to live on their salary. This argument may be weaker if the PLC operates in a low-margin, high-volume sector where the cost savings from non-financial methods outweigh any productivity gains, making financial incentives a more practical tool. However, other factors such as investment in training and technology may have a greater impact on productivity than either pay or non-financial methods, because they address the capability dimension: workers who lack the skills or tools to be more productive cannot simply “try harder” regardless of incentive. Therefore, the success of non-financial methods will depend on whether the PLC has already satisfied the basic pay threshold that makes them feasible.

Conclusion

Overall, one could argue that improved pay is a necessary condition for productivity improvement but is not the best method in most contexts for a large PLC.

While both approaches have merit, the most significant factor that needs to be considered is the nature of the work, as its impact outweighs other considerations to a greater extent, particularly in determining whether pay-for-output (effective for measurable, repetitive tasks) or non-financial motivation (effective for knowledge and creative roles) is the superior lever.

However, this depends on the current pay level, because a PLC where pay is already competitive gains very little from further pay increases, whereas a PLC with below-market wages will find that pay improvement is a prerequisite for any other motivational strategy. It also depends on the competitive strategy (Porter’s Strategies) — a cost leader like Amazon uses pay-for-output; a differentiator like Google uses autonomy and enrichment.

But ultimately, the combination of competitive pay and Herzberg motivators carries greater weight than either approach alone, because pay prevents dissatisfaction while enrichment creates genuine motivation, and productivity gains from both sources are additive. The most significant risk is that a PLC relies on pay alone and builds a workforce that is financially incentivised but intrinsically disengaged — vulnerable to being poached by competitors and incapable of the innovation that drives long-term growth.

Therefore, improved pay is not the best method in isolation — it is a foundation upon which more effective non-financial and capability-building strategies must be built, meaning that pay is necessary but non-financial motivation and training outweigh it as the most effective path to sustained productivity improvement for a large PLC.

Essay 2: “To what extent is delegation likely to increase workforce productivity?” [25] — 2017 P1 Q22

Model Answer Guidance

Introduction — Standard Version

Delegation involves passing decision-making authority (though not overall responsibility) from managers to subordinates. One could argue that this increases productivity by enhancing employee motivation, developing skills and reducing management bottlenecks — but the extent depends on the quality of leadership, the nature of the work, and whether employees are equipped and willing to accept greater authority.

Introduction — PBA Challenge Version

P: One could argue that delegation is likely to increase workforce productivity, because it provides employees with greater responsibility and autonomy — which (Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory) identifies as genuine motivators that drive sustained engagement and higher output, unlike financial incentives which deliver only temporary gains. B: However, delegation can reduce productivity if employees lack the skills or confidence to handle the delegated authority effectively, leading to poor decisions, costly errors and the need for managerial intervention — suggesting the effect depends heavily on the workforce’s capability and the manager’s ability to delegate appropriately. A: This essay will argue that delegation is likely to increase productivity for skilled, motivated employees in appropriate roles, but may reduce it in others — meaning the effect is strongly conditional rather than universal.

Argument One (FOR Delegation Increasing Productivity) — Version A: Explicit Chains of Analysis

One could argue that delegation is likely to significantly increase workforce productivity, this means that when employees are trusted with greater authority, they experience increased responsibility and autonomy — motivators in (Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory) — leading to genuine engagement rather than mere compliance. Therefore, motivated employees produce more, make fewer errors and show greater initiative in solving problems, as a result output per worker rises as both speed and quality of work improve. This could lead to significant and sustainable productivity gains, as evidenced by John Lewis Partnership, where employees (known as Partners) are empowered to make decisions within their roles, contributing to consistently high customer service scores and market-leading productivity per employee in retail — outperforming competitors who rely on closer supervision and tighter control. The empowerment of partners also satisfies (Maslow’s) esteem and self-actualisation needs, further reinforcing motivation and retention. Furthermore, delegation frees senior managers from routine operational decisions, causing them to focus on strategic tasks where their time and expertise add the most value, multiplying the productivity impact across the organisation through more effective leadership and better strategic decision-making (Tannenbaum Schmidt Continuum). This could lead to a widening span of control without loss of effectiveness, reducing management costs and enabling the business to operate with a flatter structure and lower overhead costs, improving overall efficiency. This thus leads to the conclusion that delegation generates productivity gains at multiple levels simultaneously — motivated frontline workers and more effective managers — suggesting that delegation is likely to significantly increase workforce productivity in organisations where the conditions for effective delegation are met.

Evaluation of Argument One

However, this may not be the case if the employees to whom authority is delegated lack the skills, experience or confidence to exercise it effectively. This is likely to depend on the training and development investment the business has made — delegation amplifies the capability of a well-trained workforce but magnifies the errors of an undertrained one. However, this assumes that all employees welcome greater responsibility, when in reality (Maslow’s) framework suggests that employees whose security or social needs are unmet may find increased responsibility threatening rather than motivating, resisting delegation and performing less effectively as a result.

Argument One (FOR Delegation Increasing Productivity) — Version B: Implicit Chains of Analysis

One might suggest that delegation generates its most powerful productivity effects through the motivational mechanism (Herzberg) identifies as the heart of genuine engagement: responsibility. Unlike pay, which motivates briefly before becoming expected, responsibility creates an intrinsic commitment to the work that sustains performance over time — because the employee has personal ownership of the outcome. John Lewis Partnership illustrates this at organisational scale: by treating all employees as Partners with genuine decision-making authority and profit-sharing stakes, the company builds a workforce whose personal and financial interests are aligned with business performance, generating productivity and customer satisfaction metrics that consistently outperform traditionally managed retail competitors. (Maslow’s) esteem and self-actualisation needs are simultaneously addressed, creating a reinforcing motivational environment. The structural benefits compound the individual-level effects: as delegation widens spans of control, the organisation becomes flatter, communication faster and management costs lower — a structural productivity gain independent of the motivational one. For businesses pursuing a differentiation strategy (Porter’s Strategies) where customer experience, innovation and service quality are the competitive differentiators, the productivity gains from delegation — better decisions, faster responses, more engaged frontline workers — are likely to be substantially greater than those achievable through financial incentives alone.

Argument Two (AGAINST — Delegation May Not Increase Productivity) — Version A: Explicit Chains of Analysis

On the other hand, one could contend that delegation is unlikely to increase productivity in all circumstances and may in fact reduce it, this means that when authority is delegated to employees who lack the necessary skills, knowledge or experience, the quality of decision-making deteriorates, (Scientific Decision Making) frameworks are bypassed, and costly errors increase. Therefore, managers who delegate prematurely or without adequate training investment may find that operational errors, customer complaints and rework costs rise faster than any productivity gains from increased motivation, as a result the net effect on output per worker is negative, particularly in regulated industries such as healthcare, aviation or financial services where errors carry serious legal and reputational consequences. This could lead to a situation where delegation increases variance in output quality rather than overall output volume, meaning that average productivity appears similar but consistency falls — a particularly damaging outcome for businesses competing on reliability (Porter’s Strategies — cost leadership) where predictable performance is the product. Furthermore, not all employees respond positively to increased responsibility: workers in (Taylor’s) traditional manufacturing roles who are accustomed to close supervision and piece-rate pay may perceive delegation as an additional burden without additional financial reward, causing resentment rather than engagement and further reducing productivity. This could lead to increased labour turnover as workers leave for roles that better match their preference for clear instruction and financial incentive, further undermining productivity through replacement costs and skill loss. This thus leads to the recognition that the productivity impact of delegation is strongly conditional on the workforce type, the quality of training, and the leadership capability of the delegating manager — suggesting that delegation does not inevitably increase productivity and may reduce it when conditions are unfavourable.

Evaluation of Argument Two

However, the impact may be limited because the risks of poor delegation are manageable through progressive skill development and careful matching of authority to capability — the key is not whether to delegate but how and to whom. This could be less significant if the business invests in training before delegating, mitigating the competence risk. However, other factors such as the organisational culture may have a greater impact: a power culture (Handy’s Culture) in which a dominant leader has historically made all decisions creates deep-rooted resistance to delegation that training alone cannot overcome, meaning the effectiveness of this strategy will largely depend on whether the culture can be transformed to support it.

Conclusion

Overall, one could argue that delegation is likely to increase workforce productivity for the majority of businesses, but the extent varies significantly with context.

While both arguments have merit, the most significant factor that needs to be considered is the capability and readiness of the workforce, as its impact outweighs other considerations to a greater extent, particularly in determining whether delegation unlocks the motivational gains (Herzberg) identifies or exposes a skills gap that reduces performance.

However, this depends on the industry, because in knowledge-intensive services, creative industries and professional roles, delegation is likely to be the single most powerful productivity lever available. In standardised manufacturing, (Taylor’s) financial incentives may be more effective than autonomy for many workers.

But ultimately, effective delegation carries greater weight than most alternative approaches because it simultaneously motivates workers, develops capability, reduces management costs and improves decision speed — generating productivity gains through multiple mechanisms at once. The benefits of delegation outweigh the risks of over-supervision in most contexts, because a workforce that is trusted to perform is inherently more engaged and productive than one that is merely compliant.

Therefore, delegation is likely to increase workforce productivity in most circumstances, but the magnitude of the gain depends on the capability of the workforce, the quality of leadership, and whether the organisational culture supports the trust that effective delegation requires, meaning that delegation increases productivity when conditions are right, but is not a universal solution.

Essay 3: “Should KMH plc change from soft to hard HRM to boost profit?” [16] — 2017 P3 Q3

Model 16-Mark Answer Guidance

Argument FOR — Hard HRM Could Boost Profit

One could argue that switching to hard HRM (Hard HRM) could increase KMH plc’s profits by directly reducing labour costs — the largest expense for most businesses. Hard HRM treats workers as a cost to be minimised, favouring zero-hours contracts, performance targets, close supervision and output-based pay systems consistent with (Taylor’s Scientific Management). For KMH plc, this could mean reducing permanent headcount through flexible contracts, implementing tighter productivity monitoring and removing non-financial benefits that add cost without guaranteed returns. This could lead to falling fixed labour costs as permanent staff are replaced with flexible contracts, reducing the break-even point (Break-Even Analysis) and improving profit margins. Furthermore, output-linked pay ensures that wages rise only when productivity justifies the cost, meaning the cost-per-unit falls as efficiency improves. Companies such as Amazon demonstrate that hard HRM approaches can generate high productivity metrics in operational environments, suggesting that for KMH plc’s manufacturing or logistics operations, a shift towards harder HR practices could deliver measurable profit improvements.

Argument AGAINST — Hard HRM May Harm Profit

On the other hand, one could contend that a switch to hard HRM is likely to damage KMH plc’s profits rather than improve them, particularly if the business relies on skilled, experienced workers whose retention and engagement are critical to quality. Hard HRM typically increases labour turnover as workers who feel undervalued and monitored seek employment elsewhere, generating significant recruitment and training costs that may exceed any wage savings achieved. (Herzberg’s Two-Factor Theory) explains the mechanism: removing the positive aspects of soft HRM (enriched roles, empowerment, development opportunities) eliminates the motivators that drive genuine productivity, leaving workers to perform at the minimum level required to retain their employment. The result is a workforce that is cheaper per hour but less effective per pound of cost — a net negative for profitability. Moreover, the reputational damage of a publicly visible shift to hard HRM practices could harm KMH plc’s employer brand, making it harder to attract quality candidates in future, creating a talent pipeline problem that compounds over time through the (Product Life Cycle) of organisational capability.

Balanced Reply & Judgement

On balance, whether hard HRM boosts profit for KMH plc depends critically on the nature of the workforce and the business model. This is likely to depend on the type of work: for routine, repetitive, measurable tasks, hard HRM and (Taylor’s) piece-rate system can deliver genuine cost savings. For skilled, knowledge-based or customer-facing roles, the loss of soft HRM’s motivational benefits is likely to reduce quality and increase turnover, harming profitability in ways that are harder to measure but ultimately more damaging. Therefore, a blanket switch to hard HRM is unlikely to be the optimal strategy for a PLC. The most effective approach combines competitive pay (addressing (Herzberg’s) hygiene requirements) with targeted output incentives for suitable roles — a hybrid model rather than a wholesale shift to either extreme. It depends on KMH plc’s strategic positioning: if cost leadership (Porter’s Strategies) is the goal, selective hard HRM elements may be appropriate; if differentiation through quality and service is the strategy, retaining soft HRM is likely to be more profitable in the long run.

Analyse: Benefits of Non-Financial Methods of Motivation [9] — 2024 P1 Q19

Model 9-Mark Answer Guidance — 7–8 Chains of Analysis Required Per Paragraph

Exemplar Paragraph 1 — Sustainable Motivation Without Rising Costs

One could argue that non-financial methods of motivation generate sustainable productivity improvements without permanently increasing the wage bill, this means that methods such as job enrichment, empowerment and team working address (Herzberg’s) genuine motivators — responsibility, achievement and meaningful work — rather than hygiene factors, creating sustained engagement that persists over time rather than fading as a pay rise quickly becomes the new expectation. Therefore, a business that invests in enriching roles and empowering employees builds a workforce that is intrinsically motivated rather than merely extrinsically compliant, as a result output per worker rises not just in volume but in quality, creativity and problem-solving capability — dimensions that financial incentives fail to capture and that create sustainable competitive advantage (Porter’s Strategies). This could lead to a significant improvement in the cost-to-productivity ratio, as the business achieves higher output per employee without the permanent cost increase that a pay rise represents — improving profit margins and strengthening competitive pricing ability. Google exemplifies this through its investment in workplace autonomy, creative projects and psychological safety rather than competing solely on salary, achieving among the highest revenue-per-employee ratios globally. This thus leads to the conclusion that non-financial methods are particularly valuable for businesses with skilled, knowledge-based or creative workforces, where the returns on motivational investment are highest and the limitations of financial incentives are most pronounced, meaning that non-financial methods often deliver superior productivity gains per pound invested compared to pay increases.

Exemplar Paragraph 2 — Reduced Labour Turnover and Associated Costs

One might also argue that non-financial methods reduce labour turnover by addressing the underlying reasons employees leave — boredom, lack of recognition, absence of development opportunities — rather than simply compensating them financially for staying, this means that employees who feel empowered, recognised and supported in their growth are significantly less likely to seek employment elsewhere, as their esteem and self-actualisation needs (Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs) are being met within their current role. Therefore, labour turnover falls, saving the substantial direct and indirect costs of replacement — recruitment advertising, agency fees, interview time, induction training and the productivity loss during the new employee’s learning curve — as a result the business retains accumulated knowledge, institutional memory and customer relationships that experienced employees represent, further enhancing productivity through accumulated competence rather than periodic disruption. This could lead to a compounding productivity benefit over time, as an experienced, stable workforce becomes progressively more efficient through learning effects, driving unit costs down and output quality up — moving the business towards a potential Cash Cow position (Boston Matrix) in its operational capabilities. John Lewis Partnership’s consistently low turnover relative to retail sector averages demonstrates this mechanism in practice, with engaged Partners delivering service quality that sustains premium pricing and customer loyalty. This thus leads to the recognition that the productivity benefit of non-financial motivation is partly direct (higher output from motivated workers) and partly indirect (lower turnover costs and retained expertise), meaning the full return on non-financial motivation investment substantially exceeds what is immediately visible in productivity metrics alone.

Analyse: Works Council Impact on HR Performance at KMH plc [12] — 2019 P3 Q2

Model 12-Mark Answer Guidance — 7–8 Chains of Analysis Required Per Paragraph

Exemplar Paragraph 1 — Improved Communication and Reduced Conflict

One could argue that setting up a works council significantly improved HR performance at KMH plc by establishing a formal channel through which employee concerns could be raised and addressed before they escalated into costly industrial disputes, this means that managers gained regular, structured access to workforce sentiment, identifying emerging issues around pay, working conditions, and job security (Maslow — Safety Needs) early enough to address them proactively rather than reactively. Therefore, the frequency and severity of disputes at KMH plc fell, as a result the days lost to strike action and work-to-rule decreased, directly improving labour productivity as workers spent more time producing output rather than organising collective resistance. This could lead to measurable improvements in key HR performance metrics including lower absenteeism rates (as engagement rose and dissatisfaction fell) and reduced labour turnover as employees felt more valued and heard within the business. Furthermore, the consultative structure of the works council enabled KMH plc to involve employee representatives in change management decisions, drawing on frontline knowledge to improve implementation quality and reduce resistance to restructuring — consistent with participation as a method of overcoming resistance (Kotter & Schlesinger). This thus leads to the conclusion that the works council functioned as both a communications tool and a conflict prevention mechanism, improving HR performance across multiple dimensions simultaneously, suggesting that the works council had a positive and multi-layered impact on HR performance at KMH plc.

Exemplar Paragraph 2 — Limits of the Works Council

One might equally argue that the works council’s impact on HR performance at KMH plc was limited by its advisory-only status, this means that unlike a trade union which can organise industrial action and legally compel negotiation, the works council can only make recommendations that management retains the right to ignore, as a result employees who felt that their concerns were heard but not acted upon became more cynical about the process over time, potentially reducing the legitimacy and effectiveness of the council as a channel for genuine engagement. This could lead to a situation where KMH plc’s labour turnover improved initially (as workers felt represented) but subsequently deteriorated if management failed to follow through on commitments discussed in council sessions — a gap between the perception and reality of involvement that damages trust more deeply than having no representative structure at all. Furthermore, if a significant proportion of KMH plc’s workforce remained trade union members alongside the works council, the two representative structures could have created confusion and conflict, with union representatives and council members offering conflicting advice and pursuing different agendas, causing management to receive contradictory workforce signals and making HR policy decisions more difficult rather than easier. This thus leads to the recognition that the works council’s impact on HR performance depended critically on whether management used it as a genuine consultation mechanism or as a tokenistic alternative to proper collective bargaining, meaning that the effect on HR performance was positive when genuinely used, but could be counterproductive if employees perceived it as a substitute for real representation.

2017 P1 Q11 — Taylor vs Herzberg: Piece Rate

Statement 1: ‘Taylor would see piece rate as increasing motivation.’
Statement 2: ‘Herzberg would see piece rate as increasing motivation.’

A. Statement 1 is true. Statement 2 is true.
B. Statement 1 is true. Statement 2 is false.  ✔
C. Statement 1 is false. Statement 2 is true.
D. Statement 1 is false. Statement 2 is false.

Correct Answer: B — S1 true, S2 false

Why this is correct: (Taylor) believed workers are motivated by money, so piece rate — which directly links pay to output — is exactly his prescription. S1 TRUE. (Herzberg) categorised pay as a HYGIENE FACTOR, not a motivator. Hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction but cannot create genuine motivation. Only motivators (responsibility, achievement, recognition) do that. S2 FALSE.

Examiner Trap
This is the most recycled motivation MCQ concept in AQA 3.6. The trap is assuming both theorists would agree on pay. They fundamentally disagree: Taylor says money motivates; Herzberg says it only prevents dissatisfaction. Know this distinction cold.
Strategy Tip
Taylor = money motivates. Herzberg = money is hygiene only. These are mutually exclusive positions. When a question asks about a financial method, ask yourself: is it linking pay to output (Taylor YES) and is it a motivator or hygiene (Herzberg: hygiene only).

2019 P1 Q5 — Labour Retention from Data

A table shows data for two factories. ‘Factory X has a higher labour retention rate AND higher labour productivity than Factory Y.’

A. Statement is true.  ✔ (verify by calculation)
B. Statement is false.
C. Factory X has higher productivity only.
D. Factory Y has higher retention only.

Correct Answer: Calculate both metrics for both factories

Why this is correct: Labour retention = 100% − labour turnover. Labour productivity = output ÷ employees. Calculate each metric for both factories from the data provided. The trap: students confuse high turnover with high retention. High turnover = LOW retention. If X has 10% turnover, retention = 90%. If Y has 8% turnover, retention = 92% — Y has higher retention, not X.

Examiner Trap
Do not read “labour turnover” data and assume it tells you about “labour retention” directly. They are inversely related. High turnover = low retention. Always convert turnover to retention (100 minus turnover) before comparing.
Strategy Tip
For data MCQs: step 1 — calculate the exact metric asked about for ALL factories. Step 2 — compare. Never trust your intuition on data questions; do the arithmetic.

2019 P1 Q14 — Taylor and Financial Motivation

“Which payment method would Taylor NOT see as financial motivation?”

A. Commission
B. Performance-related pay
C. Piece rate
D. Salary  ✔

Correct Answer: D — Salary

Why this is correct: (Taylor) believed pay must be linked to OUTPUT to motivate. Piece rate (per unit produced), commission (% of sales) and PRP (bonuses for targets) all link pay to measurable performance. A salary is a fixed annual payment regardless of how much you produce — (Taylor) would NOT consider this motivating because it provides no incentive to increase output.

Examiner Trap
The key word is “financial motivation” through Taylor’s lens. All four options are financial. The question is which one lacks the output link that Taylor requires. Salary has no output link = not motivating by Taylor’s definition.
Strategy Tip
For Taylor: the test of any payment method is whether it links pay to output. Commission = yes (per sale). Piece rate = yes (per unit). PRP = yes (per target hit). Salary = NO (fixed regardless of output). Salary always fails the Taylor test.

2020 P1 Q13 — Maslow vs Herzberg

Statement 1: ‘Maslow believed that needs must be satisfied in sequence.’
Statement 2: ‘Herzberg believed hygiene factors motivate employees.’

A. Statement 1 is true. Statement 2 is true.
B. Statement 1 is true. Statement 2 is false.  ✔
C. Statement 1 is false. Statement 2 is true.
D. Statement 1 is false. Statement 2 is false.

Correct Answer: B — S1 true, S2 false

Why this is correct: S1 TRUE: (Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs) is explicitly sequential — physiological needs must be met before safety, safety before social, social before esteem, esteem before self-actualisation. S2 FALSE: (Herzberg) said hygiene factors prevent DISSATISFACTION when present, and cause dissatisfaction when absent. They do NOT create motivation. Only motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility) do that. Hygiene factors cannot motivate — they can only de-motivate if absent.

Examiner Trap
Many students confuse “hygiene factors” with something positive. Herzberg’s hygiene factors are necessary but insufficient. A worker with great pay and a nice office is not dissatisfied — but is NOT motivated either. To motivate, you need the other category: motivators. This is the single most important Herzberg distinction for MCQs.
Strategy Tip
Herzberg MCQ shortcut: Anything in the “hygiene” category (pay, conditions, job security, relationships) = prevents dissatisfaction only. Anything in the “motivators” category (achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth) = creates motivation. These never overlap.

2022 P1 Q6 — Employee Costs as % of Turnover

“Which change would most likely increase employee costs as a percentage of turnover?”

A. A decrease in the number of employees and an increase in revenue
B. An increase in wages and an increase in revenue of the same proportion
C. A decrease in revenue with no change in employee costs  ✔
D. An increase in labour productivity with no change in wages

Correct Answer: C — Revenue falls, costs stay the same

Why this is correct: Employee costs % = employee costs ÷ revenue. If revenue FALLS and costs stay constant, the denominator shrinks while the numerator stays fixed, so the ratio rises. A is wrong (fewer employees = lower numerator; higher revenue = larger denominator — ratio falls). B is wrong (both change proportionally, so ratio stays the same). D is wrong (higher productivity reduces cost per unit, so ratio falls).

Examiner Trap
Students instinctively think this ratio rises only when wages go up. But it can rise when revenue falls, even with no change in wages. This is the counter-intuitive answer that AQA specifically tests. The ratio is a fraction — both numerator AND denominator matter.
Strategy Tip
Employee costs % = costs ÷ revenue. Rises when: (1) costs increase OR (2) revenue decreases. Falls when: (1) costs decrease OR (2) revenue increases. For any option, ask: what happens to the numerator AND denominator?

2023 P1 Q12 — Data Table: Labour Metrics

“Using the data table, which statement is correct about labour productivity, labour turnover, and employee costs as a percentage of turnover?”

A–D: Various calculated metric comparisons across years.

Correct Answer: Apply correct formula for each metric and compare

Why this is correct: Labour productivity = output ÷ employees. Labour turnover = (leavers ÷ average employed) × 100. Employee costs % = employee costs ÷ revenue × 100. Calculate each metric for each year and compare to the statement. These three formulas must be known precisely — using the wrong denominator for any one will produce the wrong answer.

Examiner Trap
2023 tested all three metrics in a single question — and two questions (Q12 and Q13) were on the same data table. Students who rush and miscalculate one metric fail both questions. Take time to calculate each metric carefully before matching to options.
Strategy Tip
For data table MCQs: write the three formulas at the top of your working. Calculate each metric systematically for each column/year. Only then match to the statement. This disciplined approach prevents the hurried errors that lose marks.

2023 P1 Q13 — Employee Costs % Between Which Years Did It Fall?

“Between which years did employee costs as a percentage of turnover fall?”

A–D: Year combinations from the data table.

Correct Answer: Calculate the ratio for each year and identify the years where it decreased

Why this is correct: Employee costs % = employee costs ÷ revenue. Calculate this ratio for EVERY year in the table. Identify where the ratio is smaller than the preceding year. This is a change-direction question, so absolute levels matter less than the direction of change between consecutive years.

Examiner Trap
Students who look at raw employee cost figures rather than the ratio will answer incorrectly. Employee costs could rise in absolute terms while the ratio falls, if revenue grows faster. Always calculate the RATIO, not the raw number.
Strategy Tip
Calculate the ratio for each year in a column. Then compare consecutive pairs: did it go up or down? This systematic approach takes 60 seconds but ensures accuracy. Never estimate or eyeball data table questions.

2024 P1 Q3 — Empowerment

Statement 1: ‘Empowerment increases employee motivation.’
Statement 2: ‘Empowerment reduces a manager’s span of control.’

A. Statement 1 is true. Statement 2 is true.
B. Statement 1 is true. Statement 2 is false.  ✔
C. Statement 1 is false. Statement 2 is true.
D. Statement 1 is false. Statement 2 is false.

Correct Answer: B — S1 true, S2 false

Why this is correct: S1 TRUE: Empowerment gives workers more decision-making authority = responsibility = (Herzberg)’s motivator. S2 FALSE: Span of control = the NUMBER of people reporting to a manager. Empowerment changes WHAT authority those people have, not HOW MANY there are. The number of direct reports is a structural feature unchanged by empowerment. If anything, empowerment makes wider spans more manageable (empowered workers need less supervision), but it does not reduce the span of control.

Examiner Trap
Students confuse empowerment (authority given to existing reports) with delegation (passing specific tasks downward) and span of control (number of direct reports). These are three distinct concepts. Empowerment: what power workers have. Delegation: what tasks are passed down. Span: how many report to a manager.
Strategy Tip
For any empowerment question: S1 (motivation) is almost always true (Herzberg motivator). The trap is usually S2, which tests whether students confuse empowerment with structural changes to span of control or hierarchy. They are independent concepts.

2025 P1 Q10 — Capacity Utilisation and Labour Productivity

“Which factory met BOTH targets: minimum 80% capacity utilisation AND minimum 2 units per employee?”

A. Factory A only
B. Factory B only
C. Factory C only  ✔ (verify by calculation from data)
D. Factory D only

Correct Answer: Calculate both metrics for every factory from the data provided

Why this is correct: Capacity utilisation = (actual output ÷ maximum capacity) × 100. Labour productivity = actual output ÷ number of employees. Calculate both for each factory. Only the factory that meets BOTH thresholds simultaneously is correct. Students who only check one condition will pick the wrong factory.

Examiner Trap
The question explicitly says “BOTH targets.” Students who identify a factory meeting the capacity utilisation target and stop checking, without verifying the labour productivity target, will select an incorrect option. Both must be verified independently.
Strategy Tip
Create a small table with columns for each metric and rows for each factory. Fill in all values, then look for the row with a ✔ in BOTH columns. This structured approach eliminates rushed errors on dual-condition questions.

2025 P1 Q13 — QAQ plc: Targets vs Actuals

“QAQ plc set targets for labour turnover, capacity utilisation, ROCE and market share. Which statement is correct about actual performance vs targets?”

A–D: Various combinations of met/not met targets.

Correct Answer: Compare each actual figure against its target for all four metrics

Why this is correct: For EACH metric, compare the actual result to the target. Note whether actual is above or below target AND whether above/below is good or bad for that specific metric. For labour turnover: lower actual = better (met target means actual ≤ target). For ROCE, market share, capacity utilisation: higher actual = better (met target means actual ≥ target). The direction of “better” differs by metric.

Examiner Trap
Labour turnover is an inverse metric: a LOWER number means better performance. Students who treat all four metrics as “higher is better” will misclassify whether the labour turnover target was met. Always check: for this specific metric, does higher or lower mean better?
Strategy Tip
Positive metrics (higher = better): ROCE, market share, capacity utilisation, labour productivity. Inverse metrics (lower = better): labour turnover, absenteeism, cost per unit. For targets questions, determine the “direction of good” for each metric before comparing.

Full Working

Standard Calculations

Annual output: 50,000 units. Employees: 25. Total labour costs: £625,000.

Labour cost per unit = £625,000 ÷ 50,000 = £12.50 per unit

Labour Turnover Calculation

Start of year: 120 employees. End of year: 130 employees. Number who left during the year: 15.

Average employed = (120 + 130) ÷ 2 = 125

Labour turnover = (15 ÷ 125) × 100 = 12%

Labour retention rate = 100% − 12% = 88%
