Paper 2 — Preparing for the exam

AQA A-Level Business (7132/2) · Past papers 2017–2025 with question-by-question guidance

This page covers every Paper 2 sat since the specification began. For each year you can download the paper and then work through the data, the traps, the hidden models, and how to approach each question.

Paper 2 is 2 hours, 100 marks. It always has three sections, each built around a different business context. Every section gives you data (figures, tables, charts, appendices) and then asks questions that get progressively harder — from short calculations and explanations through to 9-mark analysis and 16- or 20-mark evaluation essays.

How Paper 2 works

Structure

Paper 2 always has three sections. Each section is built around a different business. The paper tests topics from Units 3.4–3.6 of the specification: decision-making, influences on business, and assessing competitiveness.

SectionWhat it coversMarksRough time
Section 1Data-heavy case study — financial ratios, calculations, data interpretation, then a big evaluation question using the appendices~34~40 min
Section 2Second business context — a mix of explain, analyse and evaluate questions with less data~34~40 min
Section 3Third business context — similar structure to Section 2, often with a broader "all businesses" essay at the end~32~40 min

What separates a top-grade answer on Paper 2

  1. Use the data — don't just quote it. If revenue went from £46,994k to £47,569k, don't just say "revenue increased." Calculate the percentage change (1.2%) and explain what it means in context. AQA rewards calculations, not descriptions.
  2. Read the question precisely. If it says "operating profit margin," don't write about operating profit. If it says "legislation," don't write about ethics. If it says "shareholders," don't write about stakeholders in general. Students lose marks every year on this.
  3. Triangulate the appendices. Section 1 usually gives you 4–6 appendices. The best answers cross-reference them — for instance, linking a falling profit margin (Appendix A) to rising input costs (Appendix B) and competitive pressure (Appendix C). Don't treat each appendix in isolation.
  4. On 9-mark questions, write one well-developed argument. Don't try to cover three points briefly. One detailed paragraph with 6–7 connected chains, two pieces of data where a table is provided, and a concluding sentence that starts "Therefore..." and links back to the question.
  5. On 16-mark and 20-mark questions, evaluate properly. Your conclusion must make a judgement about which argument is most significant, what the answer depends on, and whether that factor outweighs the counterargument. Don't just repeat your earlier points. Don't introduce new analysis in your conclusion.
  6. On "all businesses" questions, use external examples. If the question says "for all businesses" or "for any business," you need to discuss businesses beyond the one in the case study. Answers that only use the case study business are capped.
  7. Weave in business models where they fit — but accurately. If you use Herzberg, remember that pay is a hygiene factor, not a motivator. If you use Porter's Five Forces, explain the mechanism. If you use Ansoff, apply it to the specific growth strategy in the case study. Don't just name-drop theories.
Common mistakes that cost marks nationally:
  • Confusing cash flow with profit
  • Writing about operating profit when the question says operating profit margin
  • Confusing economies of scale with economies of scope
  • Defaulting to B2C thinking when the business sells to other businesses (B2B)
  • Forgetting currency symbols and units on calculations
  • Reverse percentage errors (subtracting 10% instead of dividing by 1.1)
  • ARR errors (forgetting to subtract initial cost before dividing by number of years)

Time management

You have 120 minutes for 100 marks. That's roughly 1 minute 12 seconds per mark. A 16-mark question deserves about 19 minutes. A 3-mark calculation deserves about 4 minutes. Don't spend 15 minutes on a 4-mark question.

1SCFUK-based manufacturer of computer components. Sells to UK and EU businesses. Falling sales and margins. New CEO plans to move production to Spain.
2Seebus plcFood retailer with 110 UK stores. Higher quality positioning. JIT inventory. Rising employee dissatisfaction and labour turnover.
3DodoSmall, growing low-cost airline. UK to Europe flights. 5,000 permanent staff. Low-cost business model based on asset utilisation.
Section 1 — SCF: Data analysis and question breakdown

The data you're given

  • Appendix A: Cash flow forecast for Oct–Nov 2025. Closing balance of £4.30m at end of Oct. Inflows and outflows given. The twist: customers will be given one month's credit from 1 November.
  • Appendix B: Financial data 2022–2024. Inventory turnover falling (5.9 → 5.6 → 5.2). Operating profit falling (£0.92m → £0.71m → £0.63m). Non-current assets rising. Capital employed rising.
  • Appendix C: Market data showing total UK and EU market sales 2020–2028 (forecasts from 2026). EU market growing strongly. UK market shrinking. SCF's EU sales growing but UK sales declining.
  • Appendix D: Decision tree. Option 1: Upgrade UK factory (£2m cost, 0.7 success probability, £5m/£2m payoffs). Option 2: Build Spain factory (£9m cost, 0.4 success probability, £30m/£1m payoffs). Do nothing: £0m.
  • Appendix E: Economic forecasts 2025–2028. UK GDP growth low (0.2–1.3%). EU GDP growth much higher (3.1–5.1%). UK unemployment rising. UK interest rates 5.2–6.1%. Pound weakening against euro (€1.18 → €1.00).

What the story tells you

SCF is a business in decline domestically but with a clear opportunity in Europe. The data is deliberately set up to create a tension: the Spain move is risky (high cost, low probability of success) but the EU market is growing fast while the UK market is shrinking. The economic data supports the move — EU GDP growth is 3–5 times UK growth, and the weakening pound makes UK exports more price competitive but also makes operating costs in Spain cheaper when paid in euros from sterling revenues.

Hidden models: Porter's Five Forces (buyer power increasing because of mergers reducing number of buyers), Ansoff Matrix (market development — same products, new geographic market), decision trees, investment appraisal, exchange rate impact on international competitiveness.

Question by question

Q1.1 — Calculate SCF's closing balance for November 3 marks

The trap here is the credit terms. Previously all customers paid immediately. Now they get one month's credit from 1 November. So November's inflows become £0 from customers (they won't pay until December) but you still have October's customers paying in November (the £3.35m was already forecast assuming immediate payment). Actually, the question says "all customers take advantage of the new payment terms" — so November inflows = October inflows (£3.24m, from customers who ordered in October and are now paying one month later), November outflows = £3.22m. Opening balance = £4.30m + (£3.24m − £3.11m) = £4.43m. Then November: £4.43m + £3.24m − £3.22m = £4.45m.

Check: Show your workings clearly. Include the £ sign.

Q1.2 — Explain one possible reason for the change in inventory turnover 4 marks

Inventory turnover has fallen from 5.9 to 5.2 times. This means SCF is holding stock for longer. Possible reasons: falling sales mean finished goods sit in the warehouse longer; the business may be building up buffer stock because of supply chain concerns (the case study mentions mergers reducing the number of buyers, which could also affect supplier relationships); or efficiency has been declining as the case study states.

Q1.3 — Using Appendix D and other information, analyse why SCF chose Option 2 9 marks

Calculate the expected values:

  • Option 1: (0.7 × £5m) + (0.3 × £2m) = £3.5m + £0.6m = £4.1m − £2m cost = £2.1m net gain
  • Option 2: (0.4 × £30m) + (0.6 × £1m) = £12m + £0.6m = £12.6m − £9m cost = £3.6m net gain

Option 2 has a higher expected value. But you also need qualitative reasoning: the EU market is growing 3–5 times faster than the UK market, some manufacturers will only buy components made in the EU, and the falling pound makes exporting from a euro-zone base more attractive. SCF's UK sales are forecast to decline even without the move. The new CEO's vision is for "ambitious growth" — Option 1 (upgrading the UK factory) doesn't address the structural problem that buyers are consolidating and preferring EU-based suppliers.

Q1.4 — To what extent do the changes in the economic environment create more threats than opportunities for SCF? 16 marks

Threats from the data: UK GDP growth is sluggish (0.2–1.3%), UK unemployment is rising (limiting domestic demand), interest rates are high (5.2–6.1% — making borrowing expensive for the Spain investment), and the pound is weakening (increasing costs of any imported materials).

Opportunities from the data: EU GDP growth is strong (3.1–5.1%, accelerating), a weaker pound makes UK-manufactured goods cheaper for EU buyers (improving price competitiveness), and if SCF moves to Spain it benefits from EU single market access and growing EU demand.

For evaluation: The answer depends on whether SCF stays in the UK or moves to Spain. If it stays, the economic environment is largely threatening — weak domestic demand, high borrowing costs, and a declining UK market. If it moves to Spain, the same environment actually creates opportunities — the strong EU economy drives demand, and being inside the EU removes trade barriers. The most significant factor is probably the EU GDP growth because SCF's future revenue depends on winning EU contracts, which is where the market is growing.

Section 2 — Seebus plc: Question breakdown

The story

Seebus is a premium food retailer being squeezed: competitors have matched its pay rates, employees are unhappy about shift changes and supervision, and labour turnover has jumped. The CEO wants to fix motivation with career development plans and training — but hasn't addressed the pay gap or working conditions.

Q2.1 — Calculate the rate of labour turnover 3 marks

Labour turnover = (Number of staff leaving / Average number employed) × 100 = (960 / 6000) × 100 = 16%. Show the formula, the working, and the answer with a % sign.

Q2.2 — Explain one reason why Seebus uses JIT 6 marks

Seebus sells food — perishable goods. JIT means stock arrives just before it's needed, reducing waste from unsold items going past their sell-by date. For a food retailer competing on quality, fresh deliveries also mean better quality products on shelves. This supports their differentiation strategy. Link to context: "The company focuses on a smaller range of products" — which makes JIT more manageable than for a supermarket carrying 30,000 product lines.

Q2.3 — Use Herzberg's theory to analyse why Ajinder's plan may not improve motivation 9 marks

The trap: Students who say "pay is a motivator according to Herzberg" will lose marks. Pay is a hygiene factor in Herzberg's theory. Its absence causes dissatisfaction, but improving it does not motivate. This is the single most common theory error in the exam.

Ajinder's plan offers career development and training — these are motivators in Herzberg's theory. But the problem is that employees are unhappy about pay and working conditions (shift patterns, supervision). These are hygiene factors. Herzberg's theory says you must fix hygiene factors first — if employees feel underpaid relative to competitors and are unhappy with how they're supervised, offering training won't fix the underlying dissatisfaction. The motivators (career development) only work once the hygiene factors are no longer causing dissatisfaction. So Ajinder's plan addresses the wrong level of the problem.

Q2.4 — Should all businesses segment their markets based on income? 16 marks

This is an "all businesses" question — you need external examples. Income segmentation works well for Seebus (premium positioning targeting higher earners), luxury brands, and financial services. But it's less useful for businesses selling necessities (utilities, basic groceries), digital services with flat pricing (streaming), or B2B companies where purchasing decisions are based on specifications rather than income. Your evaluation should consider that income segmentation is one of several bases (geographic, demographic, behavioural, psychographic) and the best one depends on what the business sells and who buys it.

Section 3 — Dodo: Question breakdown

The story

Dodo is a low-cost airline — think Ryanair or easyJet. Its competitive advantage comes from asset utilisation (quick turnaround = more flights per day), cheaper airports, direct bookings (no commission), and unbundling (food, bags, seat choice as extras). The questions focus on HR objectives, quality measures, and whether asset utilisation is the best route to low-cost advantage.

Q3.1 — Analyse why Dodo sets HR objectives for number of employees and employee engagement 9 marks

Airlines have complex staffing: pilots and engineers need months of training, so you can't recruit quickly if you're short. Setting targets for employee numbers helps workforce planning — if Dodo knows it needs X pilots for its planned routes, it can start recruitment and training well in advance. Employee engagement matters because staff (especially cabin crew) are the customer-facing element of the service — disengaged staff deliver poor service, which damages the brand. Also, many staff work away from HQ, making engagement harder to maintain. Link to context: "Dodo sees its staff as key to the quality of service."

Q3.2 — Analyse how Dodo would benefit from being best for its quality measures 9 marks

Punctuality and low complaints directly affect repeat bookings. If Dodo has the highest percentage of on-time flights, it differentiates itself from other budget airlines where delays are common. Fewer complaints means less cost handling them (staff time, compensation) and better online reviews, which is where budget travellers make decisions. For a low-cost airline, reputation for reliability can be the deciding factor between competitors who all offer similar prices.

Q3.3 — To what extent is using non-current assets as fully as possible the best way to gain low-cost advantage? 16 marks

This is an "all businesses" question. For Dodo, maximising asset use (quick turnarounds, more flights per day) directly reduces unit costs because the fixed cost of each plane is spread across more flights. But for other businesses, low-cost advantage might come from different sources: economies of scale (a large manufacturer), lean production (a car company), low labour costs (outsourcing), technology (automation replacing manual work), or supply chain efficiency. Your evaluation should argue that asset utilisation is important for capital-intensive industries (airlines, hotels, manufacturing) but less relevant for service businesses with low fixed assets (consultancies, digital businesses). The most significant factor depends on the industry's cost structure.

Section 1 — Biggs Ltd: Data analysis and question breakdown

The data you're given

  • Appendix A: Financial statements 2022–2023. Revenue up slightly (£46,994k → £47,569k). Gross profit down (£22,820k → £22,289k). Operating profit down (£4,701k → £4,650k). Payables down (£5,221k → £5,056k).
  • Appendix B: Labour productivity indices. Biggs: 104.2 (2020) → 100.3 (2023). Industry: 104.3 → 105.9. Biggs is declining while the industry is improving.
  • Appendix C: Marketing expenditure as % of revenue. Biggs: 12.7% → 27.4% (2024 forecast). Market leader: 22.4% → 24%. Industry average: 18.7% → 18.4%. Biggs massively increasing marketing spend.
  • Appendix D: Market research (only 27 interviews, conducted in 2021). Children strongly interested in new products. Over-36s much less interested.
  • Appendix E: Snack purchase vs consumption data. 17–35 year olds make 53% of purchases but consume 43%. Children consume 46% but only make 19% of purchases.

What the story tells you

Biggs has a cost problem (potato prices up 35%, gross margin falling) and a productivity problem (getting worse while competitors improve). Their response is to launch new products aimed at children through expensive sports sponsorship. But the market research is weak (only 27 interviews, done 3 years ago) and the data shows that children consume snacks but don't buy them — parents do.

Q1.1 — Calculate Biggs Ltd's payables days ratio for 2023 4 marks

Payables days = (Payables / Cost of sales) × 365. Cost of sales = Revenue − Gross profit = £47,569k − £22,289k = £25,280k. Payables days = (£5,056k / £25,280k) × 365 = 73 days. Show formula, workings, answer with "days."

Q1.2 — Explain one reason why gross profit margin fell to 46.9% 3 marks

Potato prices rose by 35% over two years. Potatoes are a key ingredient. This increases cost of sales. If Biggs cannot pass the full cost increase on to consumers (likely given the competitive market with 6 dominant firms), the gross profit margin falls. Note: the question says "fell to 46.9%" — you can verify: 22,289/47,569 = 46.85%, confirming the figure.

Q1.3 — Analyse how changes in labour productivity may cause Biggs' market share to fall 9 marks

Biggs' labour productivity fell from 104.2 to 100.3 (below the 2017 base). The industry average rose to 105.9. This means each Biggs worker produces less output relative to competitors. Higher labour costs per unit → higher total unit costs → either higher prices (losing price-competitive customers in a market dominated by 6 firms) or lower margins (reducing funds for marketing and product development). Either way, Biggs becomes less competitive. Competitors with improving productivity can afford to invest more in marketing and NPD while maintaining margins, pulling market share away from Biggs.

Q1.4 — Evaluate whether Biggs' sports sponsorship is a good way to promote its new products 16 marks

The data trap: Appendix D (the market research) is based on only 27 interviews, conducted in 2021 — three years before the launch. This is a tiny, outdated sample. Appendix E shows that 6–16 year olds consume 46% of snacks but only make 19% of purchases — so the new products need to appeal to the buyers (17–35 year olds) as well as the consumers (children).

Arguments for: The promotional campaign improves brand perception across all age groups (8.0–8.3 scores). Children are enthusiastic about the new products (9.9 score). Sports events reach families. The marketing spend increase (to 27.4% of revenue) shows serious commitment.

Arguments against: The research has serious limitations (27 interviews, 3 years old). Marketing spend is nearly double the industry average — this will further squeeze margins that are already under pressure from rising potato costs and falling productivity. The 36+ age group (who may make purchasing decisions for families) scored only 1.8 on intent to try new products.

Evaluation: The answer depends on whether Biggs can convert children's enthusiasm into actual purchases by parents. The most significant concern is probably the cost: spending 27.4% of revenue on marketing when gross margins are already falling and productivity is declining may not be sustainable.

Section 2 — Soundcheck plc: Question breakdown

The story

Soundcheck moved from Position 1 (low price, low perceived value — free service) to Position 4 (differentiation — paid service with better quality) on Bowman's strategic clock. Note: the exam uses Bowman's strategic clock here, but the current AQA spec has replaced it with Porter's Generic Strategies. If a question references a specific model shown in the paper, use it as presented.

Q2.1 — Analyse why Soundcheck changed its positioning strategy 9 marks

At Position 1 (low price/low value), revenue came only from advertisers. But costs were rising significantly and the company lost £26m in 2023. Moving to Position 4 adds subscription revenue from paying customers who get better audio, no ads, and exclusive content. This diversifies revenue away from advertising alone. The streaming market is also increasingly competitive with new entrants — some of whom own their content and can offer the free tier more cheaply. By differentiating through quality and exclusives, Soundcheck creates reasons for customers to stay rather than switching to a free competitor.

Q2.2 — Analyse difficulties turning the 2023 loss into a 2024 profit 9 marks

The £26m loss happened during a global economic slowdown. Costs have been "rising significantly" — including guaranteed salaries of £36,000+ (above industry average). Labour turnover remains high despite high pay, meaning ongoing recruitment and training costs. New competitors with lower costs (they own content rights) are entering both the paid and free markets. To turn a profit, Soundcheck needs to either significantly grow subscribers or cut costs, and both are difficult: a slowdown reduces discretionary spending (streaming subscriptions), and cutting staff costs is hard with trade union agreements in place.

Q2.3 — To what extent does consultation with trade unions enable all businesses to overcome resistance to strategic change? 16 marks

Use Kotter and Schlesinger's strategies for managing resistance: education and communication, participation and involvement, facilitation and support, negotiation and agreement (this is where union consultation fits), manipulation/co-optation, and explicit/implicit coercion. Union consultation is one tool among several. It works well for Soundcheck (staff are "supportive of strategic change") but may not work for all businesses — not all businesses have unionised workforces, and some changes (redundancies, relocation) may not be negotiable. You need external examples: a tech startup with no union, a small business where the owner just decides.

Section 3 — WWH Ltd: Question breakdown

Q3.1 — Analyse how WWH Ltd could improve its cashflow 9 marks

Current cash flow structure: 25% deposit on order, 75% on fitting, but fitting takes 2 months. So there's a long gap between manufacture (costs incurred) and final payment. Options: increase the deposit percentage (e.g. 50/50), ask for stage payments, reduce the fitting lead time (hire more fitters), negotiate longer payment terms with suppliers. The case study says WWH owns its buildings — so sale and leaseback is possible to release capital. Banks won't lend more, so debt is not an option.

Q3.2 — Analyse how organisational design may change if WWH develops a product portfolio 9 marks

Currently: functional structure, flat, no R&D department, simple. Moving to multiple products would require: R&D capability (new department or function), possibly a product-based divisional structure instead of functional, more layers of management to coordinate different product teams, and new marketing expertise for different markets if diversifying into unrelated products. The flat structure might need to become taller, increasing overhead costs but improving coordination.

Q3.3 — To what extent is environmental legislation always damaging to business? 16 marks

For WWH, legislation is threatening — it could ban their entire product. But for other businesses, environmental legislation creates opportunities: renewable energy companies benefit from emissions targets, recycling businesses benefit from packaging regulations, electric vehicle manufacturers benefit from combustion engine bans. Even for businesses that face costs (fitting filters, changing materials), first movers can gain competitive advantage. Your evaluation: legislation is damaging in the short term for businesses that must change, but can create long-term opportunities and level the playing field (everyone faces the same rules). The most significant factor is whether the business's product is the target of the legislation or just affected by it.

Section 1 — AJS plc: Data analysis and question breakdown

Key data points

  • NPV calculation: cash flows of (£500k), (£50k), £250k, £600k with 10% discount factors
  • Non-recyclable packaging market declining (1250 → 1050 thousand units, 2016–2022)
  • Recyclable packaging market booming (50 → 260 thousand units, 2016–2022)
  • HR data much worse than industry: 15% labour turnover vs 4% industry, 25 days lost to industrial action vs 5, only 14 applicants per vacancy vs 55
  • Share price fell 15% while top 100 companies rose 8%
  • Gearing at 22% — relatively low

Q1.1 — Calculate NPV 4 marks

Year 0: −£500k × 1.00 = −£500k. Year 1: −£50k × 0.91 = −£45.5k. Year 2: £250k × 0.83 = £207.5k. Year 3: £600k × 0.75 = £450k. NPV = −500 − 45.5 + 207.5 + 450 = £112k (positive, so worth investing on financial grounds alone).

Q1.2 — Calculate ROCE 4 marks

ROCE = (Operating profit / Capital employed) × 100. Capital employed = Total assets − Current liabilities = (550k + non-current assets implied from gearing) or you can use: Non-current liabilities + Equity. From the data: Gearing = 22%, NCL = £4m, so Capital employed = NCL / Gearing = £4m / 0.22 = £18.18m. ROCE = (£3m / £18.18m) × 100 = 16.5%.

Q1.3 — Analyse why AJS decided to become more capital intensive 9 marks

The HR data is terrible: 15% labour turnover (nearly 4 times industry), 25 days lost to industrial action (5 times industry), and only 14 applicants per vacancy (a quarter of the industry average). Becoming more capital intensive reduces reliance on a workforce that is difficult to recruit, expensive to replace, and frequently absent through strikes. Technology can increase consistency and output without the ongoing HR problems. Labour costs are also 40% of turnover (vs 35% industry) — capital investment could reduce this proportion.

Q1.4 — Would you advise a potential investor to buy shares in AJS? 16 marks

The data is deliberately mixed. Against investing: share price fell 15% while the market rose 8%, the core market (non-recyclable packaging) is in long-term decline, HR metrics are poor, and the brand has reputational risk from selling non-recyclable products in an increasingly environmentally conscious market. For investing: NPV is positive (£112k), ROCE is reasonable at 16.5%, gearing is low (22%) meaning capacity to borrow, current ratio is healthy (£550k/£270k = 2.04), and the investment in new technology could improve productivity. Evaluation: the most significant factor is the structural decline of the non-recyclable packaging market. Unless AJS pivots to recyclable packaging (where the market grew from 50 to 260 thousand units), the long-term outlook is poor regardless of financial ratios.

Section 2 — Homeworking: Question breakdown

Q2.1 — Calculate percentage change in people working from home 3 marks

2020: 36% of 32.5m = 11.7m. 2019: 8.7m. Change = (11.7 − 8.7) / 8.7 × 100 = 34.5%.

Q2.2 — Analyse one way the balance sheet might be affected by more homeworking 6 marks

If the business reduces office space, non-current assets fall (selling or ending leases on buildings). This reduces total assets and, if the proceeds are used to pay off debt, also reduces non-current liabilities. If the business sells owned property, it receives cash (current assets rise temporarily). Net assets might stay the same if proceeds equal the book value, or there could be a profit/loss on disposal.

Q2.3 — Analyse the impact of homeworking on how managers fulfil their role 9 marks

Managers traditionally monitor performance through direct observation. With homeworking, they need to shift to output-based measurement (what did you produce?) rather than input-based (were you at your desk?). This changes the controlling function. The leading function also changes — motivating remote workers requires different approaches (regular check-ins, virtual team building). Communication shifts from informal (watercooler conversations) to formal (scheduled calls, emails), which can slow decision-making but also create better documentation.

Q2.4 — Would increased homeworking improve performance for businesses using Elkington's Triple Bottom Line? 16 marks

People: Mixed — better work-life balance but potential isolation, harder to build team culture. Planet: Positive — fewer commuting journeys reduces carbon emissions, less office energy use. Profit: Mixed — lower property costs but potentially lower productivity if employees are distracted, plus investment needed in technology. This is an "all businesses" question — homeworking works well in sectors like IT and finance but is impossible in transportation, manufacturing, and retail. The answer depends on the nature of the work.

Section 3 — Simply Bake plc: Question breakdown

Q3.1 — Explain why APO's survey may not represent UK consumers 4 marks

The sample of 100 was drawn from APO's own 400 members — these are people who already oppose palm oil, so the sample is biased. It's not representative of the general UK population. The sample size is also small relative to the UK population.

Q3.2 — Explain how stakeholder mapping would help Simply Bake respond to APO 4 marks

Stakeholder mapping (the AQA term — not "Mendelow's Matrix") plots stakeholders by power and interest. APO has high interest but low power (small group, only 400 members). But Greenpeace and WWF have high interest and high power (large memberships, media influence). Supermarket customers are increasingly interested. This helps Simply Bake decide that APO alone isn't the threat — the larger campaign groups and customer pressure from supermarkets are the real drivers of change.

Q3.3 — Using Lewin's force field analysis, analyse why Simply Bake decided to remove palm oil 9 marks

Driving forces (for change): High-profile media campaigns, supermarket customer enquiries increasing, Greenpeace and WWF campaigns, reputational risk. Restraining forces (against change): Alternative ingredients are difficult and expensive to find, palm oil is cheap (allowing them to meet demand for cheap food). The driving forces became stronger over time (APO criticism in 2018, growing customer enquiries by 2019, media campaigns intensifying). The decision was made in 2020 with a 5-year timeline to 2025 — suggesting the restraining forces (cost and difficulty) were acknowledged but the driving forces had become too strong to resist.

Q3.4 — To what extent will businesses only act responsibly if forced by governments? 16 marks

Simply Bake removed palm oil without government legislation — driven by pressure groups and customer demand. This suggests businesses can be driven to act responsibly by market forces and reputational concerns, not just laws. But counter-examples exist: many businesses continued polluting until emissions regulations forced them to stop. Some businesses adopt CSR proactively because it builds brand value (e.g. Patagonia, The Body Shop). Others only respond to regulation. The answer depends on the visibility of the issue to consumers, the cost of acting responsibly, and the competitive environment. The most significant factor is probably consumer awareness — when customers care and can see the issue, businesses are more likely to act voluntarily.

Section 1 — UK tea market: Data analysis and question breakdown

Key data

  • Black tea PED = −1.2 (price elastic — a price rise loses more revenue than it gains)
  • Health tea PED = −0.5 (price inelastic — a price rise increases revenue)
  • Pukka growing fastest (+15.4%), PG Tips declining most (−7.2%)
  • Health tea is a growing niche; 62% of consumers say ethical sourcing is important
  • 18–24 year olds drink less tea; over-65s drink more; women more likely to drink health tea

Q1.4 — Analyse how PED data affects pricing decisions for black tea vs health tea 9 marks

Black tea is price elastic (−1.2): a 10% price rise causes a 12% fall in demand, so revenue falls. To increase sales revenue from black tea, you should reduce prices — the increase in demand more than offsets the lower price. Health tea is price inelastic (−0.5): a 10% price rise only causes a 5% fall in demand, so revenue rises. To increase sales revenue from health tea, you should increase prices. The different elasticities mean the business should adopt different pricing strategies for each product type. This matters because it affects which products the business should invest in and promote.

Q1.5 — To what extent does the information suggest now is a good time to launch a new tea brand? 16 marks

The overall tea market is declining. But the health tea niche is growing and has inelastic demand (meaning higher margins are possible). Pukka grew 15.4% by focusing on organic and ethical teas. Consumer research shows 62% value ethical sourcing. So launching a new health/ethical tea brand could work despite the overall decline. Against: the top 4 brands have high combined market share (calculate this from Appendix B), creating barriers to entry. Twinings and Taylors are already in health tea. Breaking through established brands requires significant marketing investment. The answer depends on where in the market you're entering — black tea would be a poor choice, but a niche ethical health tea brand targeting younger female consumers has data to support it.

Section 2 — Ocado & M&S venture: Question breakdown

Q2.3 — Using Maslow's theory, analyse the impact on motivation of senior managers 9 marks

Senior managers from both businesses working together on a £1.5bn venture could satisfy higher-level needs. Esteem: leading a high-profile project gives recognition and status. Self-actualisation: the creative challenge of integrating two businesses and building something new. But Maslow's hierarchy says lower needs must be met first — if managers fear for job security (safety needs) because roles may be duplicated, higher-level motivation won't work. The key mechanism in Maslow is the hierarchy itself, not just listing the levels.

Q2.4 — Are ventures a good way for all businesses to grow? 16 marks

For M&S and Ocado, combining M&S's brand and product range with Ocado's technology and distribution makes sense — each brings something the other lacks. But ventures can fail: conflicts over strategy, cultural clashes, shared profits. Not all businesses have complementary partners available. Small businesses may lack the resources to contribute equally. Alternative growth methods: organic growth (slower but full control), takeover (more expensive but full ownership), franchising. The answer depends on the strategic fit between partners and whether the shared control is acceptable.

Section 3 — DT (circuit boards): Question breakdown

Q3.1–3.3 — Inventory control chart calculations

From the chart: Maximum inventory = 6,000. Buffer stock = 750. Reorder level = 2,150. Lead time: the gap between the reorder point and delivery arriving. Read from the x-axis. Reorder quantity = Max − Buffer = 6,000 − 750 = 5,250 units. Daily usage: stock falls from 6,000 to 750 (a drop of 5,250) over a measurable number of days from the chart.

Q3.4 — Explain one impact of a fall in the pound on DT's sales 4 marks

DT exports all output to Europe and sources components in the UK. A weaker pound makes its products cheaper for EU buyers (prices in euros fall), potentially increasing demand and sales volume. At the same time, UK-sourced components don't become more expensive (they're domestic). So a falling pound is good for DT's competitiveness.

Q3.5 — Analyse how contingency planning might have helped DT 9 marks

DT had no contingency plan. When its supplier closed, it couldn't find alternatives quickly enough to fill existing orders. The new supplier was more expensive with higher defects. A contingency plan would have identified alternative suppliers in advance, negotiated standby agreements, or maintained a larger buffer stock of critical components. This would have reduced the disruption to production, maintained quality standards, and avoided the cost increase from the emergency supplier switch.

Q3.6 — To what extent is reliability the most important factor in choosing a supplier? 16 marks

For DT, reliability was critical — the supplier's sudden closure devastated the business. But other factors matter too: price (affects cost competitiveness), quality (DT's new supplier had higher defects), flexibility (can the supplier respond to changes in demand?), ethical sourcing (increasingly important to customers and regulators). This is an "all businesses" question: for a JIT manufacturer, reliability is essential. For a business with large buffer stocks, price might matter more. For a premium brand, quality is paramount. The answer depends on the business's operating model and competitive strategy.

There's a 20% probability that the pound rises 10% above forecast. With 70% of customers overseas, a stronger pound makes UK hotels more expensive for foreign visitors, reducing demand. Income elasticity = +2 means demand is very sensitive to income changes. Sensitivity analysis helps managers understand how risky the investment is — if the pound does strengthen, the demand forecasts underlying the £25m NPV would be too optimistic.

Q1.3 — Analyse HR challenges from booking patterns 9 marks

Bookings vary massively by month (peak in summer, low in winter) and by day (Friday–Saturday highest, midweek lowest). This creates workforce planning challenges: if you staff for peak demand, you overspend during quiet periods. If you staff for average demand, service quality falls during peaks. Solutions include flexible contracts, zero-hour contracts, seasonal workers — but these may affect motivation and service quality. The objective is efficiency, but the new CEO comes from an upmarket background where service quality matters.

For: ARR of 12% exceeds the 8% cost of borrowing, NPV is positive at £25m. The CEO's background in upmarket hotels explains the strategy but Drake's expertise is in budget hotels. The sensitivity analysis shows there's a 20% chance the pound strengthens, which would reduce demand from overseas visitors who make up 70% of the target market. Section 2 — Nari: Question breakdown

Q2.1 — Carroll's CSR pyramid 3 marks

The highest level of Carroll's pyramid is philanthropic responsibility — going beyond what's required to contribute positively to society. Nari invests in local communities, donates to charities, and finances environmental initiatives. This goes beyond legal and ethical obligations.

Q2.2 — How marketing managers might respond to protectionism 5 marks

European governments have limited how many phones Nari can sell. Marketing responses: focus on higher-value models (if quantity is capped, maximise revenue per unit), shift marketing to non-European markets (Asia is 55% of sales and has more room to grow), develop brand awareness so that within the quota, Nari phones are the ones consumers want.

Q2.3 — Analyse ways JIT might affect Nari's profits 9 marks

JIT with components from 50+ countries is high-risk. Benefits: lower storage costs, less capital tied up in stock, reduced waste from obsolescence (phones change every few months). Risks: any disruption in the supply chain (customs delays, shipping problems, supplier failure) stops production. Nari promises 48-hour lead times to retailers with penalties for delays — so a JIT failure doesn't just increase costs, it triggers price discounts to customers. The profit impact depends on how reliable the global supply chain is.

Q2.4 — To what extent is Taylor's theory a good way to motivate all employees? 16 marks

Taylor's theory uses financial incentives (piece rate, performance-related pay) based on the idea that workers are primarily motivated by money. This may work in Nari's supplier factories (repetitive manufacturing tasks where output is measurable). But it ignores Maslow's higher-level needs and Herzberg's motivators (recognition, responsibility, achievement). For creative roles, knowledge workers, or service jobs, Taylor's approach may actually demotivate. External examples: Amazon warehouses use Taylor-style monitoring and targets; Google uses Herzberg-style motivators (interesting work, autonomy). The answer depends on the nature of the work and the employees.

Section 3 — Zani PLC: Question breakdown

Q3.1 — Define venture capital 2 marks

Finance provided by investors (venture capitalists) to start-up or early-stage businesses in exchange for equity (a share of the business). It carries high risk but potentially high returns for the investor.

Q3.3 — Analyse why PED for Zani's products is −0.4 9 marks

PED of −0.4 means demand is price inelastic. Reasons: Zani's products are luxury jewellery with strong brand reputation and unique designs — there are few close substitutes. Customers are high-income earners for whom the price is a smaller proportion of income. Jewellery purchases are often emotional (gifts, celebrations) where price is less decisive. The unique designs create brand loyalty and reduce the ability to switch to competitors.

Q3.4 — To what extent is franchising a good way for any business to grow? 16 marks

For Zani, franchising allowed rapid growth (150 franchises worldwide) without the capital investment of opening 150 company-owned stores. Benefits: franchisees fund their own stores, have local market knowledge, and are motivated by owning their business. Risks: quality control is harder (can Zani ensure all 150 stores maintain the premium brand?), franchisees keep a share of profits, and Zani loses some control over the customer experience. For other businesses: franchising works for standardised models (McDonald's, Subway) but is harder for businesses requiring specialist skills or heavy customisation. Not all businesses have a brand worth franchising. The answer depends on whether the business model is easily replicable and whether the brand is strong enough to attract franchisees.

Correlation doesn't prove causation. Other factors could explain the sales decline: changing consumer preferences (health trends), competition from alternative breakfast options, economic conditions affecting spending, or the advertising might have become more efficient (digital vs traditional).

Q1.2 — How value of sales can increase while volume falls 2 marks

If the average price per unit increases more than the fall in volume, total value rises. This can happen through premiumisation (launching more expensive products) or price increases exceeding the fall in quantity demanded.

Q1.4 — Analyse how combined market share of top 4 affects threat of entry 9 marks

This is a Porter's Five Forces question (threat of new entrants). High combined market share of existing firms creates barriers: established brands have strong customer loyalty, economies of scale in production and distribution, control over shelf space in supermarkets, and large advertising budgets. New entrants would need significant investment to compete, and would struggle to win shelf space from established brands. However, the health tea niche shows that small, focused entrants can find space in growing sub-segments.

Q1.5 — Will market research guarantee high sales for Kellogg's new cereal? 16 marks

No research can guarantee sales. The data shows consumer preferences and trends, but: tastes change between research and launch, competitors may respond, the sample may not be representative, and execution (packaging, pricing, distribution, marketing) matters as much as the idea. Market research reduces risk but doesn't eliminate it. The answer depends on the quality of the research, the competitive response, and whether Kellogg's executes the launch effectively.

Section 2 — WeRide plc: Question breakdown

Q2.1 — Impact of electric bicycle strategy on market capitalisation 9 marks

Market capitalisation = share price × number of shares. Moving into electric bicycles could increase share price if investors believe it offers long-term growth (urban mobility trend, sustainability). But the CEO admits short-term profits will be damaged, and profit margins on bicycle rides are lower than taxi rides. Investors concerned about dividends may sell shares, pushing the price down. Net effect depends on whether the market sees this as a visionary pivot or a risky gamble.

Q2.2 — Analyse one advantage and one disadvantage of flexible contracts 9 marks

Advantage: demand for taxis varies by time of day and location. Flexible contracts let WeRide scale drivers up/down to match demand, keeping labour costs aligned with revenue. Disadvantage: flexible workers may feel less committed to the company, provide inconsistent service quality, and may work for competing platforms simultaneously — reducing WeRide's competitive advantage.

Q2.3 — To what extent do legal changes create more threats than opportunities? 16 marks

For WeRide, legal changes are threatening: licence limits reduce growth, safety bans remove them from some cities. But legal changes can create opportunities for other businesses: the electric bicycle market benefits from congestion charges and emission zones. For established businesses, regulation can be a barrier to new entrants (compliance costs are proportionally higher for smaller firms). The answer depends on the business's ability to adapt and whether the regulation targets their product or their competitors'.

Section 3 — Galin plc: Question breakdown

Q3.1 — Analyse how R&D and patents help Galin achieve high margins 9 marks

R&D (3% of revenue, triple the industry average) creates innovative products that competitors can't easily copy. Patents (55,000) provide legal protection, preventing competitors from replicating products for years. This means Galin can charge premium prices because customers can't get the same product elsewhere. The combination creates sustainable differentiation — a Porter's generic strategy of differentiation supported by ongoing innovation.

Q3.2 — Analyse why Galin chose regional over functional structure 9 marks

Galin operates in 6 regions with "major differences" in market conditions, labour markets, and economic factors. A functional structure (marketing, finance, operations as separate global departments) would struggle to respond to these regional differences. A regional structure puts decision-making closer to local markets, allowing product ranges, pricing, and marketing to be tailored. The trade-off is potential duplication of functions across regions, but the benefits of local responsiveness outweigh this for a business operating in such diverse markets.

Q3.3 — To what extent will attempts to increase labour productivity be welcomed by employees? 16 marks

Employees might welcome productivity improvements if they lead to profit-sharing, better equipment, or skills development. But productivity drives often mean: more pressure/intensity, automation replacing jobs, reduced breaks, or redundancies. The reaction depends on how productivity is increased (technology vs working harder), how gains are shared (better pay or just higher profits), and the existing relationship between management and workers. External examples: automation in manufacturing (threatening for workers), training programmes (generally welcomed), performance monitoring (often resisted).

Q1.3 — Using Figure 6 data, explain how discounters manage HR effectively 9 marks

Calculate and compare:

Q1.4 — Should the big four adopt the discounter model? 20 marks

Evaluation: the most significant factor is probably that the big four have built their businesses around a fundamentally different model. A full switch would destroy their existing competitive advantages. It makes more sense to defend against discounters through selective measures (price-matching, own-brand improvements, cost reduction) while maintaining the differentiation that keeps their core customers.

If demand and/or prices rise, revenue increases. If costs are kept low through 3D printing technology, profit margins improve. Q2.2 — Analyse effects of interest rate rise on car manufacturers' profits 9 marks

Cars are usually bought on credit, often with manufacturer finance. Higher interest rates increase the cost of borrowing for consumers — reducing demand for new cars (especially at current 78% capacity utilisation). Falling demand would push utilisation lower, increasing unit costs and squeezing margins further.

Q2.3 — To what extent is price cutting a good response to falling capacity utilisation? 16 marks

For car manufacturers, price cutting might increase volume and utilisation, spreading fixed costs over more units. Alternative responses: reduce capacity (close factories), diversify into related markets, innovate to create demand for new types of vehicle, or focus on niche markets less affected by overcapacity. The answer depends on the level of competition, the elasticity of demand, and whether the overcapacity is temporary or structural. For industries with high fixed costs (car manufacturing), price cutting can be a rational short-term response, but it's destructive if everyone does it simultaneously.
